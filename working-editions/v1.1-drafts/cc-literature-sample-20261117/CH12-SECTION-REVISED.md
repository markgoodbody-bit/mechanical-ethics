- **A conceptual correction.** v1.0 treats the jam as many contributions that could, in principle, be divided into "relative causal contributions" (lines 456, 462). The revision separates the **ordered** parts of a situation, where the book's routes and clocks work, from the **complex** pattern, which comes from parts responding to each other. It does so at the point of use, citing the Cynefin framework.
- **Practical direction.** For the complex part, the reader gets steps to follow: map the constraints, judge the effort and time to change each one, set a watch on what is out of reach, contain what could shift fast, then act in small, watched, reversible steps with an owner and a working stop. This is cited to Snowden's estuarine mapping (2022).
- **Kept and strengthened.** Responsibility still follows duty and control, not causal share. ## Revised text

# 12. When Nobody Holds the Whole Wheel

Some results are made by many people and chosen by none of them. A morning traffic jam, a rise in rents and a river that floods more often than it used to each emerge from a great many actions, and nobody decided on the whole. This is **distributed causation**. The library has already shown a small version: managers set the hours, staff run the building, a contractor maintains the entrance, a bus company sets its own timetable, and a student tries to fit them together. A missed opportunity can come from the join even when each part's own record looks fine.

So far this book has mostly followed chains. A report travels to someone with a key; a payment arrives before or after a loss; one crew can reach one house before another. That works when the pieces stay put long enough to be traced. Many situations are not like that, and this is the chapter where the language has to say so plainly.

**Two kinds of situation.** Dave Snowden's Cynefin framework draws the line we need. In *ordered* systems, cause and effect are clear or can be found by analysis. In *complex* systems, "the only way to understand the system is to interact" with it. The framework rests on what it calls bounded applicability: few methods work everywhere, but many work well within their limits (Cynefin wiki, 2021). That is a fair description of this book's tools. The route, the clock and the stages of correction in Chapter 6 belong to the ordered parts of a situation: a locked door, a repair crew, a payment date. They are the wrong instrument for the parts where the pattern comes from people and things responding to each other.

During the morning rush, commuters, delivery drivers and hospital staff set off for their different destinations. Rain begins early, a delivery van breaks down near a junction narrowed by roadworks, and the traffic thickens. Some of this is ordered. The van has a recovery time. The roadworks have a permit, an owner and an end date. The signals run to a timing plan that somebody set. Each of those can be traced, and its clock compared with the time people have.

The jam itself is different. Every driver reacts to the drivers around them, to the radio and to a routing app that is reacting to all of them at once. Change the start time of one school and the queue forms somewhere else. The delay is not a sum of a thousand small contributions. When effects depend on how the parts interact, there is no single correct way to divide the result back into shares. Asking which contributions caused the jam treats a complex pattern as if it were an ordered chain. A better question is: **what holds this pattern in place, and which of those things can anyone change?**

**Map the constraints, not the causes.** Snowden's estuarine mapping starts from that question. It treats constraints, the things that hold a pattern in place or make it possible, as the principal part of a complex system that anyone can actually manage (Snowden, 2022). The method is practical, and Snowden presents it as working at every level, from a software project to a whole strategy. First, list the constraints in play: the narrowed carriageway, the permit hours, the signal timings, the school start times, the bus lane, the app's preference for short cuts. Then ask of each how much effort it would take to change, and how long. Some things will not move within the time anyone is planning for, such as the river under the bridge or the width of an old street. Mark them as out of reach for now, and set a watch in case they begin to shift. Others could change quickly and do real damage: a residential street that becomes the app's favourite short cut overnight. Those need containment now, not a study.

**Act in small, watched steps.** In a complex situation you find out what works by trying it. Estuarine mapping recommends many small interventions rather than one large project, so that success and failure both teach something, and a direction of travel, "more like this, fewer like those", rather than a fixed target (Snowden, 2022). At the junction that might mean moving the roadworks' hours for a week, changing one signal phase, or asking the app provider to stop sending traffic down one street. Each needs somebody watching what happens and the authority to undo it. Keep what helps and stop what harms. This is not a lesser kind of planning. It is planning that expects the situation to answer back.

The ordered tools still matter inside this work. A small trial needs an owner, a way to stop it and someone who checks that stopping it actually works, just as Chapter 6 asked of every correction. A watched trial whose results reach nobody with authority is correction theatre on a smaller scale.

**Responsibility follows control and duty, not causal shares.** Ordinary speech says "the city did this" or "the system failed". Those phrases can locate a problem, but they can also hide the people who could act. A road authority can change the signal timings. A contractor holds the permit and its conditions. A transport agency chose a timetable that leaves no accessible connection. Each of these is a constraint that somebody controls, and that is where questions of duty belong. A driver who joined the queue contributed to the jam but has no power over the junction. Whether anybody was at fault still needs evidence about what they knew and did, and who should repair or compensate depends on the applicable law. In a complex pattern there may be no single correct set of causal shares to estimate. Even where an investigation can estimate them, a larger share is **not** a larger share of blame. Keeping the causal picture separate lets it serve those inquiries instead of short-circuiting them.

When nobody controls the constraints that matter, say so. The injured person should not be sent round five departments in a circle. That is a **governance gap**: authority, money and a safe complaint route need to be brought together under somebody accountable.

The same approach works beyond the road. A family may lose its home through interest rates, wages, a landlord's decision, planning rules and the law, and nobody chose the whole of it. Mapping the constraints asks which of them someone could loosen in time, such as a payment holiday, a delay to an eviction or a planning decision, and who holds each one. It does not pretend that the market could be rerun with one cause taken out.

A river is shaped by rainfall, soil, plants, farming, temperature and building. Part of that process has no chooser at all. Yet decisions about a dam, a discharge permit or a flood wall change what households and habitats can do for decades, and people not yet born will live with them. Here constraint mapping meets questions of standing. A woodland does not file a complaint, and a future generation cannot consent. Ecological science, public law, indigenous stewardship, democratic institutions and moral deliberation have their own ways of representing those interests. This language should carry their findings into the account, not replace them.
