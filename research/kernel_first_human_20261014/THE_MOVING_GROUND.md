# Mechanical Ethics: The Moving Ground

*An AI-assisted, kernel-first, complete human-book research candidate for Mechanical Ethics v0.9. Not Mark-authored, not accepted, not released. 14 October 2026.*

## To a reader living with harm

If a system is harming you now, nothing in this book asks you to put yourself in further danger to document it. You may need medical, legal, housing, family, employment or other competent help more urgently than you need a theory. A record is not rescue. You owe no system, researcher or reader your private history as proof.

# 1. Before a Choice

Put a glass of water on a table and leave it alone.

Nothing dramatic happens. Dust settles. The water changes temperature. Light moves across the glass. A tiny bubble finds the surface, or perhaps no visible movement occurs at all. The table keeps carrying the weight. Outside, a cloud crosses the sun.

We tend to begin stories with somebody choosing something. But the world does not wait for a chooser before it begins to move.

A seed in a pot will sprout or fail according to conditions that neither the seed nor its owner can fully direct. Bread rises. Metal rusts. A wound heals, or does not. Two people grow closer while they share work; two others lose contact without either making a single decisive announcement. The changes are not all continuous in a technical sense. Some are abrupt, uncertain or apparently still between events. The simpler point is that the passage of time and the occurrence of events are not under the command of the person thinking about them.

Imagine that you are the person beside the table. Before we decide what kind of being you are, give this story a boundary. There is the portion of the world we mean by *you*, and there are things we will, provisionally, treat as beyond that boundary. You breathe air that came from elsewhere. Your body depends on food, people and conditions you did not produce. Your skin is a useful boundary for many purposes and a poor one for others. Your obligations may extend beyond it; your memories have come through it. A boundary is a way of identifying what we are talking about, not a claim that the identified thing is self-sufficient.

We might draw a boundary around a single person, a household, a tree, a department, a river or a machine. The boundaries have different meanings. A department is not a body, a river has no board of directors, and a software service cannot be assumed to be a person because a diagram puts a box around it. Still, without identifying some part of the world, even tentatively, it becomes impossible to ask what is changing for that part.

Notice what we have not done. We have not made a moral judgement. The cooling water is not wrong. The growing seed is not necessarily good for everyone nearby. A boundary does not confer a right. It simply makes a question possible: what happens to this part of the world, and what is it connected to?

Now consider the person sitting beside the table again. You are awake, with things to do. The glass might be yours, or somebody else's. You could drink from it, move it out of a child's reach, leave it where it is or knock it over. Before you choose, the situation already has a history: someone filled the glass, somebody made the table, somebody supplied the water, and all of these acts took place in a world that was already changing.

Most of that history is hidden. It need not be recovered before you may drink. An ordinary life would be impossible if every action required an exhaustive account of its conditions. But the unseen history can become important when the action goes wrong. If the table is uneven, who knew? If the water is unsafe, where could that fact reach someone able to do anything? If the child is thirsty, what would it take for the child to reach the glass?

We do not yet have the means to answer. That is the point of beginning this small. A thing has been distinguished within a changing world; its boundaries are provisional; it is already involved in processes that are larger than itself.

The glass could also be the beginning of a gift. Someone might notice that you are tired and put it within your reach. Someone could teach a child how to carry it without spilling. The world is changing not only toward loss, but toward growth, connection and new skill.

For now we do not have to decide which changes ought to be encouraged. We only need to see that the ground beneath every later judgement is already moving.

# 2. The Window We Have

You look at the glass.

What you see is not everything there is to know about it. Your eyes give you light, colour, shape and a few hints about distance. You may infer that the water is cold because the surface has misted, although it could be something else. You might remember that you filled it an hour ago, but memory can be wrong. A person who tasted the water would have another kind of information. An instrument might detect something neither of you can sense.

We do not meet the whole world in one glance. We meet what can reach us through our particular means of noticing.

A window makes the difference visible. From one side you see a garden and the path beyond it. From another angle somebody can see behind the shed. The garden does not cease to contain the shed's far wall because you cannot see it. Nor does it become true that a person is hiding there merely because your view is incomplete. Missing knowledge is not evidence of absence; neither is it licence to fill the space with a preferred guess.

We can give a name to a position from which part of the world becomes available: an *aperture*. It need not be a pair of eyes. A hearing aid, an engineer's instrument, a council record, a telescope, a conversation or a machine receiving data can each provide an opening of a particular kind. The opening has limits. It also has strengths. The telescope sees far and cannot taste the water. A child's account may be precise about a frightening sound and uncertain about its source. A financial ledger can reveal a transfer while failing to reveal what the absence of money did to the person waiting for it.

The person, instrument or organisation is not identical to the aperture we are examining. You are more than your eyesight. A council may hold several conflicting reports. A machine may have a camera, a language model and a log, each with different blind spots. Treating a temporary access point as the whole entity invites confusion.

From these limited openings we assemble accounts of what seems to be happening. We make maps in our heads and documents for one another. Some are remarkably dependable. Others are useful until the moment a detail beyond their scope becomes consequential.

A map of a city can get you from the station to a hospital. It will not reliably tell you whether the lift is working today. A hospital appointment screen can tell you that a time has been booked. It may not tell you whether the patient can afford the journey. An excellent map should remain excellent at what it does; it should not be mistaken for all the territory it leaves out.

There is a difference between what we observed, what we inferred and what we do not know. It is worth keeping these apart without allowing the distinction to paralyse us.

Suppose you notice water gathering below the window. You can say you saw water there at noon. You can infer that rain has overflowed the gutter. You may need to look again to establish the cause. You can act on a reasonable provisional belief if there is time-sensitive reason to do so, while keeping the belief open to correction. Intellectual honesty does not require you to wait until every uncertainty disappears.

Nor does honesty require you to disclose everything. A neighbour who knows why somebody has missed work may have no permission to publish that knowledge. The fact can exist without belonging to the public. A withheld detail is not a false detail; a readable document is not automatically available for every purpose; and a person who says, "That is private," may be answering truthfully.

These distinctions can seem fussy beside a glass of water. They become less fussy when another person's future depends on an account made from partial evidence.

The positive side is already present. Because your view is limited, another person's different view can teach you something. Because a map is incomplete, it can be corrected. Because you do not have to possess the whole world to act, you can begin learning. A child who mistakes a leaf for an insect may come back to discover the difference. The error has opened a path to understanding.

No single aperture is entitled to make itself the final account. That is not a declaration that all accounts are equally good. Evidence, skill and method matter. It is a reminder that the best account we have is still an account, and there may be something beyond its window that the next decision needs.

# 3. The Hand on the Glass

Now somebody reaches for the glass.

The movement is small. Yet it introduces another distinction. Before the hand moves, more than one course is possible. The glass may be lifted, ignored, offered to somebody else, or broken. The possibilities are not unlimited. A child may be unable to lift it. A person with painful hands may need help. A glass on a high shelf is not available in the same way as one on the table.

Action takes place inside constraints. We can imagine alternatives that no actual person present is able to perform.

Suppose you decide to leave the glass where it is. You have made no movement toward it. The water nevertheless continues to warm. Someone might knock it over later. A seed beside it continues to grow or dry. *Doing nothing* is not a command that tells the surrounding world to stop.

This sounds obvious until a decision is delayed. We say, "Nothing has happened; the case is still pending." The sentence may be accurate about the case file and wrong about the life outside it. Rent comes due, illness progresses, opportunities pass, trust changes, skills grow or decay. The file is one part of the world, not the clock that governs all the rest.

It helps to distinguish a chosen refusal from other forms of inaction. A person may say, "No, I do not want to participate." Another may remain silent because the question never reached them. A third may be unable to respond because of illness or a broken connection. A machine may fail without communicating that it has failed. Treating all four as consent, refusal or indifference would invent motives not supported by the situation.

Even a clear decision is not identical to its consequence. You may intend to help a child by moving the glass closer. The child may spill it and learn to carry it next time. You may offer a friend advice that is correct in general and damaging in their particular circumstances. A government can pass a rule meant to improve safety and create a new barrier to care. Intention belongs to an actor's decision; outcome belongs to an unfolding world in which other things also act.

Some outcomes happen by chance. Some are strongly predictable. Some arise from interactions too complicated to attribute to any single chooser. The world does not become a puppet merely because somebody has an intention.

This leaves a practical question that will matter later. If you could choose again, what would count as making the same choice? You know more than you did earlier. The glass may have moved. The child may have learned. Tomorrow's choice takes place in a new situation. A plan that was sensible yesterday might now be reckless, while another plan becomes possible because somebody has built a tool or acquired a skill.

Change creates openings as well as closures. Moving the glass from a high shelf to a low table may make it reachable. Practising with an empty cup may give a child a capability they did not have last week. An apology can reopen a conversation that had become difficult. These too are consequences of action within a moving world.

The temptation is to call every opening good and every closure bad. We have not earned that conclusion. Closing a door against a winter storm can protect what is inside. Ending a dangerous relationship can remove one path while opening others. A promise can close off options worth surrendering. Whether a change deserves protection is a question that has not yet been answered.

We have reached something simpler and more reliable: a bounded observer acts, waits or refuses in a world that changes; the alternatives are constrained; and what follows is not guaranteed to match what was intended. The next step appears when we notice that the consequence does not stop at the boundary of the one who chose.

# 4. What Travels

A child carries the glass from the kitchen to the garden.

The water was drawn from a pipe. The pipe joins a system. The glass was washed by someone. The child has learned from seeing an adult carry it. In the garden, the child waters the seed and leaves the glass beside the pot.

Nothing in that small sequence happens inside one sealed life. Water, learning, memory, tools and obligations travel between positions. The child might not know where the water came from. The water company may never know which seed it reached. The people who laid the pipes may have died decades before the child was born.

We are connected by things that carry effects beyond the place where an action began.

A letter carries a promise across distance. A paycheck carries the result of labour into rent and food. A repair manual carries knowledge from an expert to someone learning. A rumour carries an accusation past the people who could check it. A record preserves part of a past event for a later decision. A road, a shared wall or an electrical cable joins places that would otherwise remain more independent.

These carriers can spread benefit as well as harm. The same shared wall can transmit noise and hold a warm home together. A school can spread an error through a badly written rule and spread capability through patient teaching. The connectedness is not itself the verdict.

The child waters the seed generously. Some water enters the soil; some runs through the pot into the saucer. If the saucer fills, the wooden shelf beneath it may stain. The intended effect and the unintended one share the same physical path for a while and then separate. From the child's aperture the useful act is visible: the soil is wet. From the carpenter's aperture the possible damage to the shelf may be visible. Neither account has to erase the other.

Now enlarge the scene. A neighbour borrows the watering can. Another neighbour has been trying to keep a communal planter alive during dry weather. Soon several people are using the same tap. Somebody suggests a rain barrel. Somebody else knows that standing water, weight and access require care. An arrangement that began with one child and one seed has acquired multiple participants, dependencies and different capacities to choose.

We should be cautious about calling the whole arrangement an entity that intends things. The neighbours may never agree on a single goal. One wants flowers, another wants shade, another worries about the bills. The planter changes because their actions are coupled, not because a communal mind has made a unified decision. Some systems have managers; many do not have a single controlling chooser.

A market moves without being a person. Traffic accumulates from thousands of journeys. An ecosystem changes through interacting organisms, weather, land use and chance. These things have structure and consequences even when no one actor can be said to have selected the whole outcome.

If we insist on finding one villain or one hero before describing a change, we will fail to understand many ordinary systems. If we deny individual responsibility because a system is complex, we will fail in a different direction. Particular people may have duties and powers within the larger arrangement, even when none of them controls it completely.

A useful account keeps both: the effects that move through a connected world and the bounded parts of that world that can actually intervene.

There is an important human limit here too. Connection is not ownership. Knowing a friend's private worry does not make it available for public use. Helping a neighbour water a plant does not entitle you to direct every decision about the garden. Caring for another person does not make their future a possession to administer.

The child returns to the kitchen. The glass is empty. The seed has been watered. A mark on the shelf may need attention. Other people have made the act possible, and more people may be affected than the child knew.

The picture is getting larger, but the first ground remains. We have not conjured a moral rule from the carrier. We have seen that consequences can propagate, that their direction can differ from an actor's purpose, and that beneficial arrangements as well as harmful ones depend on what survives and travels between beings.

# 5. The Road That Exists and the Road You Can Take

There is a library across town. It has books you would enjoy. A map shows a route.

Can you go?

Perhaps you can walk. Perhaps the bus runs only once an hour. Perhaps you have enough money but not enough time before the library closes. Perhaps the entrance has steps, or the lift is broken, or you do not know that the library will lend books without charge. Perhaps you are caring for someone and cannot leave them. The building and the map are real; so are the constraints that make the journey possible or impossible for this particular person now.

A possibility is not the same thing as a usable path.

If the route exists but you do not know about it, it may become usable when somebody tells you. If you know it but cannot afford the fare, information is not enough. If the bus is available on paper but has already left, the timetable cannot take you backward. If a friend offers a lift but may not arrive, you have a possible route with uncertainty you must carry into your plans.

The difference matters because institutions frequently describe opportunity from the position of the people who built the route. They can truthfully say a service exists, a complaint can be submitted, a job was advertised or an appeal was available. Another person may be unable to use that route at the time it matters.

We need not make every inconvenience into harm to see the distinction. A small child cannot take the same journey as an adult. A wheelchair user and a cyclist may reach the library by different paths. The same person may have more choices on Saturday than on Tuesday. Reachability depends on whose action is being considered, what resources and abilities they have, what they know, what the world is doing, and how long completion would actually take.

Even that is not the whole story.

Imagine two streets that look identical from where you stand. One has a clear passage beyond the corner. The other is blocked. You cannot see which is which. A map of possible worlds might show a successful route in each: go left in one, right in the other. But if you must decide before reaching the corner, and the two worlds look the same to you, it does not follow that you possess a policy that succeeds in either world. Possibilities available to an all-knowing observer are not automatically choices available to a bounded one.

Sometimes preparation helps. A sign placed before the corner can make the routes distinguishable. A trusted guide can supply missing information. A bridge can create a route that works whichever street is blocked. The positive lesson is powerful: usable possibility can be *built*, not only discovered or protected.

Now put time back into the journey. The library may close at five. If you arrive at five-thirty, a perfectly valid ticket and a correct address will not make the visit happen. You may go tomorrow, but the book you needed for today's class cannot be borrowed in time for that class. A later visit is a different outcome.

Other paths become stronger through time. A musician practises until playing a difficult passage no longer depends on a lucky attempt. Neighbours build trust by keeping small promises. A repaired bridge is maintained so it remains usable in future storms. The same passage of time that narrows some opportunities can deepen capability and make other opportunities more dependable.

This is why the words *open* and *closed* are not moral labels in themselves. A path can close because a person has made a meaningful commitment. A dangerous temptation may become harder to follow because someone has grown in skill or care. The hardening of a decision may protect something precious; it may also trap somebody in a loss that another actor had power to prevent.

For an important threatened path, we may have to ask two different timing questions. When will the damage become difficult or impossible to reverse in a comparable way? And when can a particular corrective action actually be completed, given the people, permissions, resources and uncertainty involved? A fast promise is not the same as a feasible intervention.

No universal clock answers this. The practical boundary may be gradual. An appeal arriving next month may restore a payment but not the train already missed. A medical treatment may have probabilities rather than a fixed deadline. An institution can revise its policy while the event that harmed someone remains part of history. Urgency alone does not establish irreversibility, and uncertainty about a deadline should not be replaced with false precision.

We are still describing changes and opportunities, not proving what ought to be saved. The useful conclusion at this stage is about the world: paths differ in whether they exist, are known, can be reached, can be afforded, can be finished in time, and can be maintained. Some become harder to undo. Others become more solid through practice and care.

If we want to speak seriously about the consequences of a choice, we must remember the future that an affected being can actually reach, not merely the future an outside observer can imagine.


# 6. When Change Matters to Someone

The library is closed when you arrive. You stand at the door with a valid ticket and nowhere to use it.

That is a description of what happened. Have you been wronged?

Not necessarily. You may have misread the opening hours. The staff may have had to close for a fire alarm. Somebody else may have needed the building for an emergency. You could be disappointed without anybody having acted badly. The same closing could prevent an accident. There may be a real conflict between your lost opportunity and another person's safety.

Up to this point we have been following what changes and which paths remain reachable. That knowledge does not, by itself, say which path deserves protection.

This is the place to be explicit about something moral language often hides. To call a change *harm* we must bring values into the description. We must say who or what is affected, what matters for that being or situation, what alternative we are comparing with, and why the comparison has authority to matter.

It is tempting to derive these answers from the mechanics we have already seen. The world changes; beings have limited views; some futures close. Therefore every closed future is a moral wrong. But that conclusion does not follow. Every fulfilled promise closes some other possibilities. Learning a skill makes certain errors less likely. A door shut against a storm can preserve a home. A predator and its prey cannot both have every preferred future protected. A public institution may have to choose between two legitimate claims with limited resources.

The mechanical facts help us see the choice. They cannot appoint the rightful chooser.

Consider a child about to run into a road. An adult closes the garden gate. The child protests because the garden path is no longer open. The adult has curtailed one possibility and may have protected several more. To judge the act we need more than a count of closed options. We need an account of the child, the risk, the adult's responsibilities, the other people who may be affected, and the kind of future the intervention is meant to protect.

There may be disagreement about the proper boundary. At what age should the child be allowed to open the gate alone? What supervision is reasonable? Whose judgement matters, and when does the child's growing independence change the answer? These are questions of standing, care, evidence, responsibility and social authority. They do not resolve themselves from the fact that time passes.

A similar problem arises with non-human life. The tree beside the library needs water and space. Its roots may damage a wall. The people maintaining the building have responsibilities, and the tree belongs to an ecosystem not represented by one human voice. Which lives and futures count? Who may represent them? The answers may come from law, ecological knowledge, community judgement and ethical disagreement. Calling all things entities does not quietly settle their standing.

We can nevertheless choose to make an ethical commitment openly. This book asks us to take seriously the effects our arrangements have on beings and environments, especially where one party holds more power to decide than another. It favours making consequential harm visible while correction is still reachable, and making power answerable to those affected. It also values the creation and preservation of meaningful possibilities. These are purposes offered for reasoned acceptance or rejection, not physical laws deduced from a cooling glass.

A reader may accept much of that commitment and disagree about its application. Two people may care about justice and differ over what a fair outcome requires. A worker's privacy can conflict with an employer's duty to investigate misconduct. A town may want both a flood defence and a living river. Parents and young people may disagree over safety and independence. A method that turns such disagreement into a single unquestionable number would conceal what ought to be argued about.

We can do better than saying that values are merely subjective noise. We can ask where a judgement came from. Is it the affected person's expressed preference? A legal obligation? A clinical judgement? A community decision? A provisional assumption made because the true preference is unavailable? Has someone with the relevant standing been excluded? A value may be well supported or deeply contested. Those differences can remain visible.

The child eventually learns to open the gate safely. The earlier protection did not have to last in the same form. An arrangement that treats yesterday's vulnerability as permanent may become a barrier to tomorrow's capability.

The positive and protective sides are joined here. We do not only ask which bad change to prevent. We ask which worthwhile futures could become usable, for whom, under which legitimate commitments and at whose cost.

Only after naming those commitments are we ready to make comparisons that deserve the name harm or benefit.

# 7. Comparing Without Hiding the Difference

Imagine the library considers extending its evening hours.

A longer opening could help students, shift workers and families who cannot visit during the day. It could also cost more in wages and energy, lengthen the journey home for staff and reduce resources for services that some other visitors need. An early trial might attract many readers. It might also reveal that people who most needed evening access still cannot use the last bus.

The proposal sounds simple if we say only that more opening hours create more opportunity. It becomes more truthful when we follow the change into several lives.

We need a comparison. But comparison with what?

One possibility is to compare the new hours with the current timetable. Another is to compare them with a different allocation of the same budget. A third is to compare them with a future in which the building closes entirely because its running costs are unsustainable. The proposal can look different under each reference. Choosing a baseline is part of the claim, not a neutral clerical step.

The period also matters. A one-week trial may create an immediate gain and hide an unsustainable staffing burden. A five-year plan may promise eventual savings while demanding more than current staff can safely carry. The same project can have a positive long-run ambition and an unjustified short-run transition. A good destination does not erase the journey people have to live through.

We must name the affected scope. Are we talking about one student, every evening visitor, the employees, the neighbourhood, or future taxpayers? Expanding the boundary can reveal previously hidden effects. It can also dissolve a person's serious injury into a comfortable average. A large total benefit does not, by itself, tell us what may permissibly be imposed on a smaller group.

Then there is the difficulty of comparing unlike things. An hour of paid labour, an hour of quiet study, a person's dignity, a wetland's health and an institution's cash reserve are not interchangeable units merely because they appear in the same report. A numerical total requires an authored way of valuing and combining them. If someone supplies that way, we should know who it was and what it excludes.

We can speak of a *harm difference* between two described paths: how much worse or better an outcome is, for some affected scope, under a stated account of value, over a stated period, compared with a stated alternative. In the research around Mechanical Ethics, this has sometimes been compressed into the symbol **ΔH**. The symbol is a research shorthand, not an ancient law, a released foundation or a machine for manufacturing objective moral verdicts.

Without the named scope, baseline, horizon, evidence and source of value, ΔH is just a decoration. With them, it can help prevent a more subtle error: shifting the basis of comparison halfway through an argument because the new result sounds more impressive.

Suppose the extended library hours are a success for visitors but a loss for two staff members whose childcare arrangements no longer work. Their burden cannot be cancelled invisibly by the phrase *community benefit*. It may be possible to redesign the rota, fund transport, recruit willing evening staff or shorten the extension. The point of bringing the difference into view is to make more constructive choices possible.

Sometimes two legitimate values still collide. A library may have to balance quiet study against a noisy community event. The same space and hour cannot serve both perfectly. A city may have to decide between spending on immediate accessibility and a longer-term flood project. The technical facts can narrow the plausible alternatives; they cannot choose the political and moral priority without some legitimate decision process.

A fair process may itself be contested. Who was invited? Who could attend? Were private reasons exposed unnecessarily? Could someone disagree without losing access to a service? Did those most affected have a voice with any consequence? None of this guarantees consensus. It helps distinguish disagreement made answerable from a verdict hidden inside the method of counting.

There are also cases in which the observable change is too small to justify the machinery of measurement. Choosing tea over coffee on an ordinary afternoon need not generate a harms ledger. A useful language should be able to say that the consequential difference is unclear or negligible and let the person get on with life.

And there are cases in which numbers can be indispensable. Public health, engineering risk, finance, climate and logistics often require careful quantitative comparison. Their specialised methods should be used by people who understand them. The portable distinctions here do not compete with probability theory or cost-benefit analysis; they remind us what a number was defined to count and what claims it cannot carry.

The library opens two evenings on a trial basis, with paid volunteers from the staff team, an accessible transport arrangement and a clear date for reconsideration. Some visitors gain new possibilities. The costs are not erased. Evidence will have to show what actually happened, including what did not work.

A considered gain is not merely a green arrow in a report. It is a change in a world of different beings, judged against explicit commitments that others can question. Making those commitments visible is an ethical improvement even when the final decision remains difficult.

# 8. What Can Travel Back

The first evening goes well. The second is quiet. A manager receives a report saying that thirty people attended over two nights.

The number may be accurate. It does not tell us whether the students who were expected to benefit arrived, why someone left early, whether the last bus was reachable, or what the staff experienced. It may reveal a useful pattern while leaving out the reason the trial was begun.

A person can act only on the account that reaches them. If the institution wants to change its next decision, it needs some way for reality to travel back into the room where decisions are made.

A visitor writes a note: the building was open, but the accessible entrance had already been locked. A staff member reports that the proposed closing time conflicts with a scheduled bus. A student says the extra hour made it possible to finish an assignment. These are not the same kind of testimony. Each is limited, and each can matter.

Here the idea of a witness becomes useful. A witness can be a person, a document, an instrument or another record that preserves something about an event for later examination. It is not an oracle. A witness may be mistaken, partial, compromised or irrelevant to the particular claim being made. Multiple records can copy one original error. Independence does not follow merely from having two files.

Suppose the visitor photographs the locked entrance. The library's door system logs the time it was secured. A third person remembers seeing someone unable to enter. If the photograph and log were retained independently before anyone knew there would be a dispute, they may support a stronger reconstruction than either alone. Even then, the evidence needs interpretation. The door could have been locked for an emergency. The photograph may not show another available entrance. The visitor might have been unable to use that other entrance. The competing accounts need to be allowed to meet.

A timestamp is evidence of what a clock recorded, within its custody and limits. A checksum can establish that the bytes being checked match a particular earlier held copy. Neither magically proves when the world event happened, whether the record is complete or whether its use is just. Independence has degrees; a witness is useful when its actual means of knowing and its control relationship fit the claim at stake.

Custody matters because records can be altered, deleted, exposed or used against the person who created them. A person in a precarious job may have strong evidence of a dangerous practice and good reasons not to place it in a manager's public folder. A person seeking medical care may need a confidential channel rather than a public accusation. A child cannot be made responsible for building an evidence archive about a situation adults should address.

The route for hearing evidence must not quietly become another source of harm.

It helps to distinguish access from authority to disclose. The library may possess security recordings without having the right to publish every visitor's movements. A file may be readable on an open website because somebody uploaded it, while its presence there says nothing about lawful or ethical reuse. Privacy is not dishonesty. Withholding a fact does not make the fact false or absent. A witness can support correction without becoming an excuse for universal surveillance.

If the library changes the closing procedure, it should preserve enough of the reason for later staff to understand it. But it may not need to preserve the student's full identity or the visitor's unrelated movements. Responsible memory is purpose-bound, not a hunger for every available detail.

Imagine that the manager receives all three reports and responds, "We have taken this seriously." The sentence may be sincere. It has not yet changed the door. A recorded confession, apology or incident review is not the same as an effective correction.

There must be some route from the affected information to a person or mechanism able to alter what happens. A clerk may be allowed to read the complaint but not to change building access. A senior manager may have the authority and no access to the original evidence. A provider may run the software while the library owns the policy. The strength of the witness alone cannot repair the connection.

Nor can an affected person's voice always be assumed to speak for every other affected person. The quiet reader and the music group may disagree about evening use. A worker can challenge an unsafe rota without having authority to decide the entire staffing budget. Legitimate standing and decision power need to be distinguished rather than granted to whoever most convincingly describes a harm.

This is why *answerability* is more demanding than the existence of a feedback form. The people who bear an outcome must, under some legitimate and usable arrangement, be able to bring consequential information to those who can examine it and change future action. A powerful actor may collect feedback only to become more efficient at exploiting customers; feedback by itself would not make that actor answerable.

The library adjusts its entrance procedure, keeps a telephone route for people unable to use its app, and appoints a staff member who can actually authorise changes to the closing process. The first two evenings have become a basis for better work. No one had to know the entire world to achieve it. Several partial accounts were able to correct a limited part of it.

The value of the record lies not in how impressive it looks but in what truthful, proportionate change it makes possible.

# 9. What Cannot Be Put Back

A student writes to the library after the trial.

The extended opening hours were useful, the student says, but the locked entrance on the first night meant missing the last session of a course. The library has now fixed the door procedure. The student is pleased that somebody listened and that others will not face the same barrier. The missed session will not return.

We are tempted to describe the problem as solved because the current arrangement has improved. We are also tempted to say that no correction was possible because the first loss cannot be erased. Both descriptions leave something out.

Correction changes what can still be changed. It does not necessarily restore the path a person actually lost.

An account can be reopened while the debt accumulated during its closure remains. A job can be reinstated while reputation, wages and trust have changed. A wall can be repaired after a child has spent a winter in a damp room. A rule can be withdrawn after people have left a community. The later improvement is real. So may be the remaining cost.

We can call that remainder *residue*. The word does not mean that every painful memory must be paid for, or that history can be reversed through better bookkeeping. It means that a correction's success must be described in relation to what it actually repairs, and that uncorrected consequences must not be hidden behind an administrative closing date.

Some effects are irreversible in a narrow physical sense. The past event cannot be made never to have happened. That does not prove that continuing harm remains forever; people can heal, relationships can change and new futures can be made. Some harms are gradually repairable, some are compensable without being reversible, and some leave an enduring loss. A serious account must let these differences stand.

Sometimes the most responsible act is to stop an intervention rather than continue it in the hope that another stage will justify the first. A medical procedure, an engineering experiment or an AI-assisted decision may have reached a point where evidence or authority is insufficient for the next move. A pause can preserve possibilities; a pause can also have costs. Whether it is warranted depends on the affected route, the clocks, the expertise and the authority involved.

Sometimes there is no effective rescue. The decision-maker is distant, the institution refuses to listen, or the relevant time has passed. It would be cruel to tell the person harmed that a better record will certainly deliver justice. Evidence can help an appeal, protect someone else or preserve an honest history. It can also fail. The witness should not be made to carry responsibility for a remedy that the powerful actor has withheld.

What remains, then, of answerability when the original loss cannot be corrected?

The people involved may still be owed an accurate account, a lawful remedy, a change to the repeated mechanism, protection against further misuse, or an honest explanation of what cannot be repaired. Which of these is owed depends on the actual responsibilities and values at stake. An institution cannot make the past good by rewriting its status field. Nor can it promise complete restitution where no one can supply it.

The person harmed may wish to stop talking. That is not proof that the matter was trivial, but neither is continued testimony always something others may demand. Privacy, rest and refusal can be ways of preserving what remains open. Care does not require possession of another person's future; it may require placing the work of correction on those with the resources and authority to carry it.

Closure itself needs judgement. Some disputes should end because continued proceedings impose more harm than they can remedy, because an affected party has chosen a legitimate exit, or because the evidence is insufficient to justify coercive action. Other files are closed too early to protect the institution from embarrassment. No single timer or form can settle the difference for every domain.

Return to the child and the glass. A broken glass cannot be made unbroken by calling a replacement the same object. A new glass may serve the child just as well, and the child may have learned to carry it. The earlier break still occurred. No ethical theory needs to make the break into an eternal injury or pretend it never happened. Honest repair can be both limited and worthwhile.

The student accepts the library's apology and decides not to pursue a further complaint. The library still changes its procedure and pays attention to the next student. Their decisions are different, and both matter.

That is a stronger ending than either a triumphant claim that every wrong has been undone or an insistence that nothing good can follow an irreversible loss.


# 10. The Things We Make Possible

The child returns to the pot on the windowsill.

A leaf has opened. The child has learned that watering matters, that too much water has consequences, and that the plant changes on its own timetable. An adult has learnt to put a tray beneath the pot instead of blaming the child for a shelf that cannot bear a spill. Somebody has taught the child what to notice, and the child has begun to notice without being told.

A small capability has appeared that was not present at the beginning.

It is worth seeing the difference between protecting an existing opportunity and making a new one. The child could always stand by the window. They could not always care for a plant. The skill is not a new object that can be handed over in one motion. It has been formed through repeated encounters, trial, correction, encouragement and conditions that allowed mistakes without unnecessary punishment.

Something similar happens when people learn a language, repair a bicycle, recover confidence after illness, become able to trust a friend, or discover a community that has room for them. Their future is not merely a list of options that were stored somewhere waiting to be unlocked. Other people, institutions, materials and histories help create what they can become able to do.

This is why an ethical inquiry that sees only loss will miss much of life.

Protecting people from immediate injury matters. So does opening space for learning, art, play, friendship, work, affection, solitude, exploration and care. A society that becomes excellent at auditing harm while leaving everyone with fewer means to create a life has achieved something incomplete. Conversely, a society that celebrates impressive new possibilities while treating present suffering as an acceptable transition cost may betray the very future it promises.

The two directions belong in the same account. A capability needs conditions in which it can form; preserving those conditions may require protection. Protection makes sense partly because there are lives and futures worth living. Creation can widen those futures; it can also impose burdens that must be seen before they become permanent.

The library's evening hours are a modest example. A student gains access to a quiet room. Over several weeks, that room becomes somewhere they can study without being interrupted. A volunteer teaches a child how to search for reliable information. A new friendship begins. These are not all consequences that the library can predict, own or claim as its performance. They are possibilities supported by an arrangement whose users may make of it more than its builders imagined.

The arrangement has to be sustained. Electricity and maintenance cost money. Staff need rest. Someone must replace books and repair the lift. The students' gains are not free just because entry to the building is free. A claimed benefit that depends indefinitely on unacknowledged or coerced labour is not an honest description of success.

Making a route available once differs from making it maintainable. A training programme may produce a skill that decays without practice. A friendship may need attention. A forest restoration project requires care through seasons when the first volunteers have moved away. The future contains the work of upkeep, and the people who will inherit that work deserve a place in the decision.

Formation is not automatically moral improvement. A person can become skilled at deception. An institution can become more efficient at excluding claimants. A machine can learn to classify people more rapidly without learning what the classification does to their lives. Repeated transitions produce durable habits and capacities; the ethical meaning of those capacities still requires the commitments we named earlier.

So we should not confuse growth with goodness. The positive question is more demanding: what valuable capacity is appearing, in whose life, under what conditions of authority and care, with what costs to others, and with which means for changing the arrangement as it develops?

That question can protect independence without requiring isolation. A student becomes more independent because teachers, public services, tools and other learners were present. A disabled person may gain autonomy through dependable assistance rather than through abandonment. A skilled engineer may depend on colleagues who are free to challenge a design. Mutual dependence is not inherently a failure of agency. Dependence becomes dangerous when one party can withdraw essential help without answer, conceal costs or make refusal impossible.

Care must therefore be distinguished from control. A parent helps a child cross a road and, as the child grows, learns when to let the child cross independently. A carer may know what a patient usually wants and still need to ask what the patient wants today. An AI assistant may provide valuable explanations while its user retains ways to question, learn without it or seek a human expert. The changing needs of the relationship matter more than preserving a fixed image of the helper as always necessary.

Imagine a town that decides to build a new public workshop, a greener street and a reliable path to the library. It might be tempted to proclaim an ideal future and silence anybody who points to a cost. It would be wiser to see imagination as the horizon that opens inquiry. The promise is not the proof. Who can reach the new place? Who will maintain it? Which other worthwhile use of the land or money will be lost? Who may leave or disagree? What evidence would make the next design better?

Good futures are not only protected, and they are not only constructed. They are also discovered, maintained, shared and transformed by people who do not all want the same life. One person wants a laboratory. Another wants a quiet garden. Another wants to travel. Another wants enough security to stay near the people they love.

The point is not to prescribe a single destiny. It is to help make worthwhile possibilities more real without making those who live among them less able to answer back.

The child has not learned a philosophical system. They have learnt to care for a plant and to notice that care changes both the plant and the carer. That is enough to continue from the windowsill toward a world larger than the room.

# 11. When Nobody Holds the Whole Wheel

On a busy morning, a thousand people set out for work.

They choose different routes. A delivery vehicle breaks down. A hospital shift changes. Rain falls sooner than forecast. Traffic thickens. A lane closes because a utility crew finds a damaged cable. The resulting delay affects people who never made a decision about that cable.

Who chose the traffic jam?

In ordinary speech we say the city did this or the system failed. Those phrases can help us locate a problem, but they can also create the illusion of a single mind making one decision. Much of the outcome is produced by interacting processes: physical constraints, individual choices, regulations, business incentives, chance and decisions made at several levels of authority.

A description that demands one identifiable chooser for every consequence will leave much of this reality unexplained.

There may still be accountable actors. A road authority may have responsibility for signs. A contractor may have failed to secure a site. A transport agency may have chosen a schedule knowing it would leave no accessible connection. Responsibility should be traced to actual capacity, duty and control, not invented for a convenient villain or dissolved into the word *system* whenever a powerful actor would rather disappear.

A market presents the same difficulty. Prices move because many participants buy, sell, wait and respond to information. Some firms and regulators can strongly affect the outcome, while no single actor chooses it in full. A family may lose housing through a chain of interest rates, wages, landlord decisions, planning constraints and law. An adequate account keeps the interacting causes visible and asks what each responsible actor could realistically have done.

The natural world is broader still. A river is changed by rainfall, soil, plants, farming, temperature and human construction. There may be no chooser at all for part of the process. Yet decisions about a dam, pollution or flood protection can alter the reachable futures of households and habitats for decades. People not yet born will inherit some of those changes and cannot attend the meeting that authorises them.

Here the language of bounded views and carried effects remains useful, but standing and representation grow more difficult. A future generation cannot give current consent. A woodland does not file a complaint. People may reasonably disagree about how to give their interests weight. Ecological science, public law, indigenous stewardship, democratic institutions and moral deliberation have methods we should not replace with the word *entity*. The question is how to keep these diverse forms of knowledge and obligation accessible within a common account of consequential change.

Now consider a computer system that sorts the thousand morning journeys into suggested routes.

It may reduce traffic and save time. Its model may not include every road closure, disability or safety concern. A driver may follow a suggested shortcut through a narrow residential street. A policy designed to optimise average journey time can impose noise and danger on residents who are not using the app and may have no way to challenge it.

The software does not need an inner wish to inconvenience them. It acts through data, objectives, infrastructure and human reliance. The people who designed it, deployed it, set permissions and retained logs may have different knowledge and different authority. Attributing everything to "the AI" can hide the people and institutions who decide what it is allowed to change.

Machine speed alters the stakes. A single flawed assumption can propagate through many decisions before anyone notices. It can also make possible improvements that manual work could never deliver. The interesting question is not whether the machine is good or bad in general, but whether this particular arrangement carries benefits and burdens honestly, maintains a usable way to correct its behaviour and preserves the authority of those entitled to decide.

A person advertised as *human in the loop* may be asked to approve too many outputs to understand any of them. That may be a ceremonial witness, not meaningful oversight. Another person who sees representative evidence, can investigate patterns and has effective power to halt a class of decisions may provide stronger answerability with fewer clicks.

Even an emergency stop is not magic. It has a scope, a response time and consequences of its own. Stopping a traffic system may send every driver toward a dangerous alternative. A good stop needs an accountable owner and an understood recovery path. A route is not usable because somebody drew a button on the screen.

As artificial systems become more capable, further questions may arise about relationship, continuity, experience, legitimate refusal and standing. Current uncertainty is not a reason to declare machines persons by analogy with humans or to close every future question by calling them mere instruments. We can say what is known about present interfaces and consequences, and leave unresolved claims openly unresolved.

At this scale, care becomes a design question as well as a personal one. Does an arrangement let its beneficiaries inspect important advice, decline participation where legitimate, preserve their knowledge if the service changes and reach accountable people when their futures are at stake? Does it unfairly shift costs to less visible entities, including workers, neighbours, non-users and the environment?

An ambitious system can be a genuine gift. It can widen knowledge, create useful work, protect people and help communities coordinate. That possibility deserves exploration. A gift becomes less valuable when accepting it requires surrendering the ability to question what it does.

We started with a hand lifting a glass. We now have many hands, no single wheel and sometimes no hand at all. The structure has widened: bounded accounts, evolving conditions, constrained interventions, interacting effects and paths that open or close. But the permissions, causal mechanics and obligations differ. The analogy does not grant a manager, a model or a theory the sovereignty of a moral law.

# 12. The Shape That Returns

The child at the windowsill looks down at the seed.

At first there was simply a pot, some soil, a glass and a change already under way. Then the child learnt to notice what could be seen and what lay outside that view. They acted, waited, changed their mind and sometimes misjudged the result. Other people supplied water, knowledge, time and materials. Some routes to growth became easier, others harder. They cared about the seed and needed to learn what its care required.

Nothing about that experience required a book.

Yet we can now recognise similar relationships elsewhere without confusing the things involved.

In a household, somebody may know a problem first while somebody else can fix it. A promise travels between people. Delay can make an apology less useful, and an improved habit can make trust grow. In a school, teachers, children and families have different knowledge and authority; a record may carry a warning, a grade or a useful observation, but an education cannot be reduced to those records. In a hospital, diagnosis, consent, evidence, risk and time are governed by professional responsibilities much stronger than those surrounding an ordinary household dispute.

In an engineering firm, an early warning may need to travel from a technician to a person with stop authority before a design is deployed. Formal tests, independent review and specialist standards matter. In a city, laws and budgets determine who can repair a crossing or create a new public service. In an AI system, data and code carry choices at speed, often to people who never agreed to participate. In a landscape, irreversible land use and slow ecological processes can change the conditions available to beings generations apart.

The scenes are not interchangeable. Treating a sick patient as a software ticket would be a grotesque error. Treating a whole society as a child to be parented would license domination. Treating a language model's output as a direct account of human experience would erase a boundary of evidence. A recursive pattern is useful only when it helps us notice relations while preserving the differences that make each domain real.

Perhaps we can call this a *middle-out* way of looking. It begins with something graspable: a change, a being, a choice, a relationship. It follows the necessary connections outward and inward. It does not insist that a complete theory must descend from above before an ordinary decision can be made, nor that the world's difficulties can be understood by looking only at tiny pieces in isolation.

There is another trap to avoid. Recognising a pattern is not proof that every instance was caused by the same mechanism. A child forgetting a promise, a council losing a report and a machine suppressing an alert may all produce an unanswered warning. The reasons, intentions, remedies and legitimate authorities can differ sharply. A shared vocabulary can help ask the next question. It cannot answer it in advance.

If our language tells us that "nothing was heard", we should ask what was expected to arrive, through which channel and by when. Silence is meaningful relative to an expected signal or duty; otherwise absence can be invented from a blank field. If we say two events corroborate one another, we must ask whether they arose independently or copied the same source. If we say an intervention prevented a harm, we need a defensible comparison with what might otherwise have happened, not only a pleasing result after the event.

These questions are not a demand that ordinary life become a permanent audit. They are safeguards for moments when the difference matters.

An unfamiliar system can sometimes be understood more quickly by asking what it can see, how it changes, who else is affected, which routes remain practical and who can revise its actions. That may reveal a hidden cost, an inaccessible appeal or a promising opportunity. At other times the correct response is to use established expertise and stop translating. Mechanics of water belong to hydrology. Diagnoses belong to competent medicine. Duties and entitlement belong to law and legitimate authority. A language that refuses to hand work back to its stronger owners becomes another dangerous bureaucracy.

The language must apply to itself.

This book draws a boundary around certain relations. It looks from an aperture: an AI-assisted author attempting to build a readable account from research and examples. It is not an unmediated view of the world. Its stories can suggest distinctions but cannot establish empirical outcomes. Its claims about moral significance depend on declared commitments, not on mathematical necessity. Its descriptions may omit something consequential. Its future revisions will have histories and burdens.

The fact that a manuscript is coherent is not proof that it is correct, humane or useful. A readable explanation can still mislead. A source test can prove that a file contains all its paragraphs while proving nothing about whether a reader understands them. An AI review can find a formal defect while missing what a human finds unbearable to read. The point of preserving a witness to the text's origin is to make correction easier, not to borrow authority from the machinery that produced it.

A framework about answerability should be answerable. That means retaining the earlier evidence when a claim changes, identifying who made a new claim, making criticism reachable and allowing a legitimate reader to reject the whole construction.

If the grammar begins demanding trust merely because it can describe many domains, it has failed its own premise.

# 13. The Room Again

Come back to the glass on the table.

The water has warmed. The light has moved. Somebody may have drunk from it or left it untouched. The seed on the sill is larger, or the soil has dried, or the pot has broken. The same room holds several futures, not all of them chosen by the person who first looked around.

At the beginning we noticed only change and a provisional boundary.

Now the room contains more than objects. It contains perspectives that cannot be made identical; accounts that may be accurate but incomplete; actions and silences with different causes; effects carried between people; paths that can be imagined but not always reached; time in which repair can become impossible or a new capability can take root.

It also contains commitments.

The fact that a life can be affected does not by itself tell us what that life is owed. We have to say what we mean by harm and benefit, who may decide, what evidence supports the claim, which alternative we are comparing, and how disagreement may be expressed without being erased. These judgements are not mechanical outputs. They are made by people and institutions with duties, knowledge and limits, sometimes on behalf of beings who cannot yet speak for themselves.

The mechanics can still help.

They can expose a promise of help that arrives after the relevant future has closed. They can reveal that a system's record is not the event it describes, or that the people suffering a consequence have no way to make their account reach power. They can remind us that a repair may be real while leaving losses it cannot undo. They can show why a protected exit, a legitimate refusal or a private room may matter as much as access to another service.

And they can turn our attention toward creation.

Not every valuable future was waiting as an option in a menu. Someone grows into a new skill. Neighbours make a place where learning can happen. A scientist discovers something useful. A city builds safer streets. A damaged habitat is tended across generations. People find relationships, beauty and time for lives they did not know were possible. These gains have clocks, carriers, costs and maintenance just as injuries do. Their value does not make their burdens disappear.

Nothing here grants the book authority to command those lives. It asks for something more modest and more difficult: to keep consequential reality visible enough that people can act with partial knowledge, change course when warranted and share responsibility without denying the differences between their positions.

There will be times when the most responsible answer is to act immediately, times to ask someone with better knowledge, times to refuse, times to protect what already exists, and times to build a capability that nobody presently has. There will be times when a route has gone and cannot be recovered. An honest account must still find ways to answer for what happened without demanding that the injured person keep proving it forever.

The moving ground cannot be frozen for our convenience. We do not get a final view from which all conflict disappears.

We can, however, notice more than we noticed at the start. We can see what an action can really change, where uncertainty belongs, who has power, whose cost is absent from the record, and what becomes possible when the people affected can answer back.

The glass is still only a glass. The child is still learning. Outside, the world has not become simple.

That is not a failure of the journey. It is the reason to continue it.
