# Mechanical Ethics: The Moving Ground

*An AI-assisted, kernel-first, complete human-book research candidate for Mechanical Ethics v0.9. Not Mark-authored, not accepted, not released. 14 October 2026.*

## To a reader living with harm

If a system is harming you now, nothing in this book asks you to put yourself in further danger to document it. You may need medical, legal, housing, family, employment or other competent help more urgently than you need a theory. A record is not rescue. You owe no system, researcher or reader your private history as proof.

# 1. Before a Choice

Put a glass of water on a table and leave it alone.

Nothing dramatic happens. Dust settles. The water changes temperature. Light moves across the glass. A tiny bubble finds the surface, or perhaps no visible movement occurs at all. The table keeps carrying the weight. Outside, a cloud crosses the sun.

We tend to begin stories with somebody choosing something. But the world does not wait for a chooser before it begins to move.

A seed in a pot will sprout or fail according to conditions that neither the seed nor its owner can fully direct. Bread rises. Metal rusts. A wound heals, or does not. Two people grow closer while they share work; two others lose contact without either making a single decisive announcement. The changes are not all continuous in a technical sense. Some are abrupt, uncertain or apparently still between events. The simpler point is that the passage of time and the occurrence of events are not under the command of the person thinking about them.

Imagine that you are the person beside the table. Before we decide what kind of being you are, give this story a boundary. There is the portion of the world we mean by *you*, and there are things we will, provisionally, treat as beyond that boundary. You breathe air that came from elsewhere. Your body depends on food, people and conditions you did not produce. Your skin is a useful boundary for many purposes and a poor one for others. Your obligations may extend beyond it; your memories have come through it. A boundary is a way of identifying what we are talking about, not a claim that the identified thing is self-sufficient.

We might draw a boundary around a single person, a household, a tree, a department, a river or a machine. The boundaries have different meanings. A department is not a body, a river has no board of directors, and a software service cannot be assumed to be a person because a diagram puts a box around it. Still, without identifying some part of the world, even tentatively, it becomes impossible to ask what is changing for that part.

Notice what we have not done. We have not made a moral judgement. The cooling water is not wrong. The growing seed is not necessarily good for everyone nearby. A boundary does not confer a right. It simply makes a question possible: what happens to this part of the world, and what is it connected to?

Now consider the person sitting beside the table again. You are awake, with things to do. The glass might be yours, or somebody else's. You could drink from it, move it out of a child's reach, leave it where it is or knock it over. Before you choose, the situation already has a history: someone filled the glass, somebody made the table, somebody supplied the water, and all of these acts took place in a world that was already changing.

Most of that history is hidden. It need not be recovered before you may drink. An ordinary life would be impossible if every action required an exhaustive account of its conditions. But the unseen history can become important when the action goes wrong. If the table is uneven, who knew? If the water is unsafe, where could that fact reach someone able to do anything? If the child is thirsty, what would it take for the child to reach the glass?

We do not yet have the means to answer. That is the point of beginning this small. A thing has been distinguished within a changing world; its boundaries are provisional; it is already involved in processes that are larger than itself.

The glass could also be the beginning of a gift. Someone might notice that you are tired and put it within your reach. Someone could teach a child how to carry it without spilling. The world is changing not only toward loss, but toward growth, connection and new skill.

For now we do not have to decide which changes ought to be encouraged. We only need to see that the ground beneath every later judgement is already moving.

# 2. The Window We Have

You look at the glass.

What you see is not everything there is to know about it. Your eyes give you light, colour, shape and a few hints about distance. You may infer that the water is cold because the surface has misted, although it could be something else. You might remember that you filled it an hour ago, but memory can be wrong. A person who tasted the water would have another kind of information. An instrument might detect something neither of you can sense.

We do not meet the whole world in one glance. We meet what can reach us through our particular means of noticing.

A window makes the difference visible. From one side you see a garden and the path beyond it. From another angle somebody can see behind the shed. The garden does not cease to contain the shed's far wall because you cannot see it. Nor does it become true that a person is hiding there merely because your view is incomplete. Missing knowledge is not evidence of absence; neither is it licence to fill the space with a preferred guess.

We can give a name to a position from which part of the world becomes available: an *aperture*. It need not be a pair of eyes. A hearing aid, an engineer's instrument, a council record, a telescope, a conversation or a machine receiving data can each provide an opening of a particular kind. The opening has limits. It also has strengths. The telescope sees far and cannot taste the water. A child's account may be precise about a frightening sound and uncertain about its source. A financial ledger can reveal a transfer while failing to reveal what the absence of money did to the person waiting for it.

The person, instrument or organisation is not identical to the aperture we are examining. You are more than your eyesight. A council may hold several conflicting reports. A machine may have a camera, a language model and a log, each with different blind spots. Treating a temporary access point as the whole entity invites confusion.

From these limited openings we assemble accounts of what seems to be happening. We make maps in our heads and documents for one another. Some are remarkably dependable. Others are useful until the moment a detail beyond their scope becomes consequential.

A map of a city can get you from the station to a hospital. It will not reliably tell you whether the lift is working today. A hospital appointment screen can tell you that a time has been booked. It may not tell you whether the patient can afford the journey. An excellent map should remain excellent at what it does; it should not be mistaken for all the territory it leaves out.

There is a difference between what we observed, what we inferred and what we do not know. It is worth keeping these apart without allowing the distinction to paralyse us.

Suppose you notice water gathering below the window. You can say you saw water there at noon. You can infer that rain has overflowed the gutter. You may need to look again to establish the cause. You can act on a reasonable provisional belief if there is time-sensitive reason to do so, while keeping the belief open to correction. Intellectual honesty does not require you to wait until every uncertainty disappears.

Nor does honesty require you to disclose everything. A neighbour who knows why somebody has missed work may have no permission to publish that knowledge. The fact can exist without belonging to the public. A withheld detail is not a false detail; a readable document is not automatically available for every purpose; and a person who says, "That is private," may be answering truthfully.

These distinctions can seem fussy beside a glass of water. They become less fussy when another person's future depends on an account made from partial evidence.

The positive side is already present. Because your view is limited, another person's different view can teach you something. Because a map is incomplete, it can be corrected. Because you do not have to possess the whole world to act, you can begin learning. A child who mistakes a leaf for an insect may come back to discover the difference. The error has opened a path to understanding.

No single aperture is entitled to make itself the final account. That is not a declaration that all accounts are equally good. Evidence, skill and method matter. It is a reminder that the best account we have is still an account, and there may be something beyond its window that the next decision needs.

# 3. The Hand on the Glass

Now somebody reaches for the glass.

The movement is small. Yet it introduces another distinction. Before the hand moves, more than one course is possible. The glass may be lifted, ignored, offered to somebody else, or broken. The possibilities are not unlimited. A child may be unable to lift it. A person with painful hands may need help. A glass on a high shelf is not available in the same way as one on the table.

Action takes place inside constraints. We can imagine alternatives that no actual person present is able to perform.

Suppose you decide to leave the glass where it is. You have made no movement toward it. The water nevertheless continues to warm. Someone might knock it over later. A seed beside it continues to grow or dry. *Doing nothing* is not a command that tells the surrounding world to stop.

This sounds obvious until a decision is delayed. We say, "Nothing has happened; the case is still pending." The sentence may be accurate about the case file and wrong about the life outside it. Rent comes due, illness progresses, opportunities pass, trust changes, skills grow or decay. The file is one part of the world, not the clock that governs all the rest.

It helps to distinguish a chosen refusal from other forms of inaction. A person may say, "No, I do not want to participate." Another may remain silent because the question never reached them. A third may be unable to respond because of illness or a broken connection. A machine may fail without communicating that it has failed. Treating all four as consent, refusal or indifference would invent motives not supported by the situation.

Even a clear decision is not identical to its consequence. You may intend to help a child by moving the glass closer. The child may spill it and learn to carry it next time. You may offer a friend advice that is correct in general and damaging in their particular circumstances. A government can pass a rule meant to improve safety and create a new barrier to care. Intention belongs to an actor's decision; outcome belongs to an unfolding world in which other things also act.

Some outcomes happen by chance. Some are strongly predictable. Some arise from interactions too complicated to attribute to any single chooser. The world does not become a puppet merely because somebody has an intention.

This leaves a practical question that will matter later. If you could choose again, what would count as making the same choice? You know more than you did earlier. The glass may have moved. The child may have learned. Tomorrow's choice takes place in a new situation. A plan that was sensible yesterday might now be reckless, while another plan becomes possible because somebody has built a tool or acquired a skill.

Change creates openings as well as closures. Moving the glass from a high shelf to a low table may make it reachable. Practising with an empty cup may give a child a capability they did not have last week. An apology can reopen a conversation that had become difficult. These too are consequences of action within a moving world.

The temptation is to call every opening good and every closure bad. We have not earned that conclusion. Closing a door against a winter storm can protect what is inside. Ending a dangerous relationship can remove one path while opening others. A promise can close off options worth surrendering. Whether a change deserves protection is a question that has not yet been answered.

We have reached something simpler and more reliable: a bounded observer acts, waits or refuses in a world that changes; the alternatives are constrained; and what follows is not guaranteed to match what was intended. The next step appears when we notice that the consequence does not stop at the boundary of the one who chose.

# 4. What Travels

A child carries the glass from the kitchen to the garden.

The water was drawn from a pipe. The pipe joins a system. The glass was washed by someone. The child has learned from seeing an adult carry it. In the garden, the child waters the seed and leaves the glass beside the pot.

Nothing in that small sequence happens inside one sealed life. Water, learning, memory, tools and obligations travel between positions. The child might not know where the water came from. The water company may never know which seed it reached. The people who laid the pipes may have died decades before the child was born.

We are connected by things that carry effects beyond the place where an action began.

A letter carries a promise across distance. A paycheck carries the result of labour into rent and food. A repair manual carries knowledge from an expert to someone learning. A rumour carries an accusation past the people who could check it. A record preserves part of a past event for a later decision. A road, a shared wall or an electrical cable joins places that would otherwise remain more independent.

These carriers can spread benefit as well as harm. The same shared wall can transmit noise and hold a warm home together. A school can spread an error through a badly written rule and spread capability through patient teaching. The connectedness is not itself the verdict.

The child waters the seed generously. Some water enters the soil; some runs through the pot into the saucer. If the saucer fills, the wooden shelf beneath it may stain. The intended effect and the unintended one share the same physical path for a while and then separate. From the child's aperture the useful act is visible: the soil is wet. From the carpenter's aperture the possible damage to the shelf may be visible. Neither account has to erase the other.

Now enlarge the scene. A neighbour borrows the watering can. Another neighbour has been trying to keep a communal planter alive during dry weather. Soon several people are using the same tap. Somebody suggests a rain barrel. Somebody else knows that standing water, weight and access require care. An arrangement that began with one child and one seed has acquired multiple participants, dependencies and different capacities to choose.

We should be cautious about calling the whole arrangement an entity that intends things. The neighbours may never agree on a single goal. One wants flowers, another wants shade, another worries about the bills. The planter changes because their actions are coupled, not because a communal mind has made a unified decision. Some systems have managers; many do not have a single controlling chooser.

A market moves without being a person. Traffic accumulates from thousands of journeys. An ecosystem changes through interacting organisms, weather, land use and chance. These things have structure and consequences even when no one actor can be said to have selected the whole outcome.

If we insist on finding one villain or one hero before describing a change, we will fail to understand many ordinary systems. If we deny individual responsibility because a system is complex, we will fail in a different direction. Particular people may have duties and powers within the larger arrangement, even when none of them controls it completely.

A useful account keeps both: the effects that move through a connected world and the bounded parts of that world that can actually intervene.

There is an important human limit here too. Connection is not ownership. Knowing a friend's private worry does not make it available for public use. Helping a neighbour water a plant does not entitle you to direct every decision about the garden. Caring for another person does not make their future a possession to administer.

The child returns to the kitchen. The glass is empty. The seed has been watered. A mark on the shelf may need attention. Other people have made the act possible, and more people may be affected than the child knew.

The picture is getting larger, but the first ground remains. We have not conjured a moral rule from the carrier. We have seen that consequences can propagate, that their direction can differ from an actor's purpose, and that beneficial arrangements as well as harmful ones depend on what survives and travels between beings.

# 5. The Road That Exists and the Road You Can Take

There is a library across town. It has books you would enjoy. A map shows a route.

Can you go?

Perhaps you can walk. Perhaps the bus runs only once an hour. Perhaps you have enough money but not enough time before the library closes. Perhaps the entrance has steps, or the lift is broken, or you do not know that the library will lend books without charge. Perhaps you are caring for someone and cannot leave them. The building and the map are real; so are the constraints that make the journey possible or impossible for this particular person now.

A possibility is not the same thing as a usable path.

If the route exists but you do not know about it, it may become usable when somebody tells you. If you know it but cannot afford the fare, information is not enough. If the bus is available on paper but has already left, the timetable cannot take you backward. If a friend offers a lift but may not arrive, you have a possible route with uncertainty you must carry into your plans.

The difference matters because institutions frequently describe opportunity from the position of the people who built the route. They can truthfully say a service exists, a complaint can be submitted, a job was advertised or an appeal was available. Another person may be unable to use that route at the time it matters.

We need not make every inconvenience into harm to see the distinction. A small child cannot take the same journey as an adult. A wheelchair user and a cyclist may reach the library by different paths. The same person may have more choices on Saturday than on Tuesday. Reachability depends on whose action is being considered, what resources and abilities they have, what they know, what the world is doing, and how long completion would actually take.

Even that is not the whole story.

Imagine two streets that look identical from where you stand. One has a clear passage beyond the corner. The other is blocked. You cannot see which is which. A map of possible worlds might show a successful route in each: go left in one, right in the other. But if you must decide before reaching the corner, and the two worlds look the same to you, it does not follow that you possess a policy that succeeds in either world. Possibilities available to an all-knowing observer are not automatically choices available to a bounded one.

Sometimes preparation helps. A sign placed before the corner can make the routes distinguishable. A trusted guide can supply missing information. A bridge can create a route that works whichever street is blocked. The positive lesson is powerful: usable possibility can be *built*, not only discovered or protected.

Now put time back into the journey. The library may close at five. If you arrive at five-thirty, a perfectly valid ticket and a correct address will not make the visit happen. You may go tomorrow, but the book you needed for today's class cannot be borrowed in time for that class. A later visit is a different outcome.

Other paths become stronger through time. A musician practises until playing a difficult passage no longer depends on a lucky attempt. Neighbours build trust by keeping small promises. A repaired bridge is maintained so it remains usable in future storms. The same passage of time that narrows some opportunities can deepen capability and make other opportunities more dependable.

This is why the words *open* and *closed* are not moral labels in themselves. A path can close because a person has made a meaningful commitment. A dangerous temptation may become harder to follow because someone has grown in skill or care. The hardening of a decision may protect something precious; it may also trap somebody in a loss that another actor had power to prevent.

For an important threatened path, we may have to ask two different timing questions. When will the damage become difficult or impossible to reverse in a comparable way? And when can a particular corrective action actually be completed, given the people, permissions, resources and uncertainty involved? A fast promise is not the same as a feasible intervention.

No universal clock answers this. The practical boundary may be gradual. An appeal arriving next month may restore a payment but not the train already missed. A medical treatment may have probabilities rather than a fixed deadline. An institution can revise its policy while the event that harmed someone remains part of history. Urgency alone does not establish irreversibility, and uncertainty about a deadline should not be replaced with false precision.

We are still describing changes and opportunities, not proving what ought to be saved. The useful conclusion at this stage is about the world: paths differ in whether they exist, are known, can be reached, can be afforded, can be finished in time, and can be maintained. Some become harder to undo. Others become more solid through practice and care.

If we want to speak seriously about the consequences of a choice, we must remember the future that an affected being can actually reach, not merely the future an outside observer can imagine.
