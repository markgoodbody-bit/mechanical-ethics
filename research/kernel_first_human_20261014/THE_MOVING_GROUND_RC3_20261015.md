# Mechanical Ethics: The Moving Ground

*A meaning-first human reading draft — Mechanical Ethics v0.9 research candidate, 15 October 2026. Edition ME09-RC3-20261015 · NOT RELEASED.*

## A note before we begin

The world changes while we make up our minds. Mechanical Ethics offers a small language for noticing those changes, understanding which choices are genuinely possible, and making correction reachable before avoidable losses become difficult to undo. The same language can help us see how people learn, cooperate and create possibilities that did not exist before.

We will start with ordinary meanings: state, change, boundary, view, account and action. Each will become useful in a scene, and then join with earlier ideas. Later, clocks, witnesses, power, responsibility and care will deepen the picture. You do not need any background in philosophy or mathematics. The point is to learn a way of asking better questions, not to memorise rules.

The approach helps us examine how situations work. It does not decide moral standing or replace the judgement of affected people, lawful decision-makers or skilled specialists. We will name those choices when the story reaches them. Notes at the end explain the draft's sources, authorship and unresolved limits.

**If you are being harmed now:** you do not need to endanger yourself or disclose private information to use any idea here. A record is not rescue; seek competent and safe help when you need it.

# 1. Before a Choice

**State** is our description of some conditions at a particular moment. It may say where a glass is, how warm its water is, who is in a room, what a person knows, or whether a door can be opened. We have selected those features because they matter to a question. A state is not the complete world, and the word does not imply that the conditions stay still.

**Change** is a relevant difference in conditions or relationships across moments. It may be gradual, abrupt, reversible, uncertain, helpful or damaging. It does not mean that every feature changes all the time. It also does not require the particular person watching to decide or act. If we compare two moments, we should say what feature we compared, on what evidence and at which times. We also need a reason to treat it as the same relevant thing or path across those moments; otherwise a supposed change may be a change of subject.

Now put a glass of water on a table. Nobody touches it. Light moves across the room, the water changes temperature, and a tiny bubble may reach the surface. The position of the glass might be steady while its temperature changes. Neither the glass nor the person looking at it controls all the processes around them. We began with meanings simple enough to carry to this scene. We have not yet decided whether any change is good or bad.

The same structure includes growth. Put a seed in a pot beside the glass. With suitable water, light, temperature and time, it may sprout. With poor conditions, it may fail. Even when nobody deliberately intervenes, the seed and its surroundings are not guaranteed to remain as they were. A child might later learn to care for the plant, making a new future possible. These are different possibilities with different conditions, not a promise that the world always improves.

We usually begin a story when someone makes a choice. But the world does not wait for a chooser before events unfold. Bread rises; metal rusts; a friendship strengthens through repeated contact or fades as people become separated. A class deadline can pass while an application is still marked pending. The file may have an unchanged administrative status, while the student's available future has changed. We must distinguish a stationary field in a record from a stationary world.

We need one further word to ask a particular question about change. **Boundary** means a line we select around part of a situation for a stated purpose. A boundary tells the reader which part of the world we are discussing. It need not be a physical wall. A body's skin is a useful boundary for some medical questions and a poor one for the social supports that keep that body alive. A household boundary might include the glass, the table and the child. A boundary around the child excludes the glass while leaving their dependence on it visible.

Choosing a boundary is an authored act of description. It may be useful, misleading, contested or revised. The person who drew the line should be able to explain why. We can draw boundaries around a person, a department, a river, a machine or a group, but these are not the same kinds of things. A department is not a body; a river does not acquire a board of directors; a software service is not granted human standing because a diagram encloses it. **Boundary is not moral standing.** Identifying something for study does not decide its rights or what is owed to it.

An **entity**, for this discussion, is the particular person, living thing, group or arrangement we are trying to follow through change. We must have a reason to treat it as continuing across the moments we compare; a name or box on a page is not enough. Some entities experience consequences without choosing the action that caused them. Describing one does not settle whether it has feelings, rights or decision authority.

Look again at the glass. Someone manufactured it; someone supplied water; an adult may have placed it beyond a child's reach for a reason. The room has a history beyond the moment we chose to observe. We cannot recover all that history before every ordinary act. But we can widen the boundary when the question demands it: who can reach the glass? Who put it there? Which conditions would make it safe for the child to carry it? What happens to the plant if nobody helps?

A modest positive act now becomes visible. Someone notices that another person is thirsty and moves the glass within reach. That is a changed condition, and it may make a desired action usable. Someone teaches a child to carry an empty cup without spilling. The child learns through trial and correction; a capacity may begin to form. We cannot yet infer an ethical duty from these physical and practical descriptions, but we can see what a gift or a lesson has to *do* in the world.

We can now ask what changed inside a chosen boundary, and what changed outside it that might matter. To learn more, we need to examine how anyone can notice and describe those changes.

# 2. The Window We Have

**Aperture** means a position or means through which part of the world becomes available for observation. A person looking through a window has one aperture; a technician using a thermometer has another. A document, a sensor or a conversation can open a different route to information. An aperture has strengths and limits. It is not the whole entity using it: a person is more than their eyesight, and an organisation may hold several conflicting accounts.

**Account** means a representation made for a purpose using observations, testimony, memory or inference. It might be a map, a medical note, a photograph, a report, a model or a remembered conversation. An account can be accurate about what it describes while omitting something essential for another decision. We should be able to say when and how it was made, what it concerns, and which claims are observed, inferred or unknown. **MODEL ≠ WORLD** is a reminder about scope, not an accusation that every model is mistaken.

You look at the glass. You see its shape and perhaps moisture on its surface. From that you infer the water may be cold. You do not know the temperature unless you obtain more information. Someone who tastes it, measures it or recalls filling it may contribute something different. None has the entire world in one glance. In this situation the observation, the inference and the unknown all have different jobs. Confusing them would make an account appear more certain than its evidence warrants.

Consider a city map. It may lead you from the station to a hospital very reliably. It might not tell you whether the lift is working today, or whether a patient can afford the journey. A good map should remain useful at the work for which it is good. It becomes misleading only when a claim is extended beyond its actual scope. A child's description of a frightening noise may be precise about the sound and uncertain about its source. An instrument may measure temperature accurately and know nothing about the fear the event caused. The method's strength does not erase the world's other features.

Now compose what we already know. A world changes across moments; an aperture exposes selected features; an account stores or carries a representation made at a certain time. Does that mean every account becomes false after a delay? No. We need an additional premise: **there is a credible reason that a consequential feature may have changed, or a consequential present-use claim lacks an adequate current warrant**. Yesterday's photograph of the glass remains evidence about yesterday, even if the water warmed today. A library's published opening hours may remain correct for months; an unexpected closure may make them unreliable for tonight.

Call that risk **possible staleness**. It is a conditional conclusion, not a universal expiry timer:

*Credible consequential change OR inadequate warrant for consequential present use → check the current claim or carry the uncertainty.*

The arrow is a practical reasoning connection, not a rule that every old record is suspect. Rechecking costs time and effort: do it when the possible change matters to the present choice, or carry a stated uncertainty instead of demanding pointless updates. A necessary check may require another aperture: a fresh observation, a second source with a genuinely independent means of knowing, or a person who can verify the door. A timestamp says when an account was recorded by a particular clock; it is not proof that the world remained the same. Nor do two copies of the same old report make two independent observations.

A partial view can also be an opening into learning. The child by the window decides a seed has failed because nothing green has appeared. Every morning they disturb the soil to see whether it is growing. Another person, who has watched several seasons of seedlings, knows that digging it up each day may prevent the very result the child wants. That person has *another view*: an account from a place and history the child does not yet share. The child may choose to hear it, test it and learn something—not merely repeat an adult's preferred answer.

A neighbour explains how to judge moisture by touching the soil beside the seed, and agrees to check it again next week if nothing emerges. There is a way to test the account without destroying the thing being watched. It takes more than two perspectives for learning to occur: the other account must be relevant, reach the child in a form they can use, and survive an appropriate check. The seed may still fail. Even then, the child has acquired a better question and a less destructive way to investigate it. No one has thereby obtained authority over the child's pot or mind.

There is a constructive relation here, not a universal law: another aperture may supply evidence or a method ours lacks, a carrier may make it available, and an actor who can test and revise their account may become more capable. We have not yet explained carriers or lasting skill; those will be earned in the next chapters. For now, limited access is not only a reason for caution. It can be a reason to listen and to share what has been learned, without demanding another person's privacy as payment.

It matters to separate access from permission to disclose. A neighbour may know why someone missed work without having permission to publish it. A nurse may have access to a medical record for treatment and no authority to put it on a public website. A missing public detail is not necessarily a missing fact; withheld is not false, and private is not deceptive. We should keep the account honest without demanding more exposure than the task justifies.

# 3. The Hand on the Glass

**Action** means an attempted intervention in a situation, undertaken by an actor with some capabilities and constraints. Action is not identical to a desired outcome. A person may choose wisely and still fail; they may act with good intent and cause damage, or have an unexpected constructive effect. To understand an action, distinguish what was selected, what was executed and what actually happened.

We also need names for what can look like doing nothing. **Null input** means no relevant command or contribution was supplied through a specified route; it does not automatically tell us why. **Waiting** may be a deliberate decision to delay, an imposed queue, or simply the passage of time. **Refusal** is an expressed or otherwise sufficiently evidenced choice not to participate. Silence by itself establishes none of these. A failed connection, a question never delivered, an inability to reply and an explicit no are different situations. Honest reasoning leaves an unknown motive unknown.

Even an expressed yes needs context: when the same person controls an essential resource, sets the terms of participation and decides the penalty for saying no, apparent consent is weak evidence of an unpressured choice. **Weak evidence of a free yes is not evidence of a no.** Where possible, ask the person what they want, make refusal less costly and check what pressures remain. Uncertainty about consent does not itself authorise a helper to override the person's expressed decision, openly or covertly. Any protective intervention requires a separately grounded duty or authority, proportionate to the situation and attentive to the person's account. Consent standards and lawful protective duties belong to the relevant domain; pressure must remain visible without turning doubt into surrogate control.

Imagine a hand reaching for the glass. The person may lift it, offer it to someone else, leave it alone or accidentally knock it over. Some routes are not genuinely possible: a child may lack the strength; an adult may have painful hands; the glass may be on a high shelf. Imagining an action is easier than having the means to perform it. We will later give that gap its own careful account.

Suppose the person leaves the glass untouched. Its temperature still changes; the seed beside it may continue to grow or dry. The absence of this person's intervention is not a command to the rest of the world to stop. The same can happen in an institution: a housing request remains pending while damp spreads. The case file has not changed its status; the room has changed. **No action does not imply no transition.** Nor does it prove that a consequential feature has changed: which effect occurred still needs evidence.

An organisation may receive no reply to a safety notice. It cannot honestly call the silence agreement unless the relevant consent arrangement supports that interpretation. Perhaps a person explicitly declined. Perhaps the message never arrived. Perhaps the system discarded the reply. The outwardly identical blank response field may call for very different corrections. The evidence of delivery, access and choice matters before anyone attributes refusal or indifference.

An achieved effect needs evidence appropriate to the claim: it may be directly **observed**, reliably **reported**, cautiously **inferred**, **disputed** or still **unknown**. Direct observation is not always possible, and intention alone does not prove the result. You might move the glass closer to help a child. The child might spill it and learn a better grip next time. A manager might issue a rule to improve safety and unintentionally block access to care. A programme may acknowledge a complaint in seconds without changing the conditions that prompted it. Distinguishing selection, execution and effect reveals where new information or changed authority could improve the next action.

The constructive possibility is not a reward for always acting. Practice can alter capabilities. Let a child try carrying a little water while someone who can help stays nearby. The adult changes the arrangement to make errors tolerable; the child acts, receives feedback and tries again. Later, if the child can carry water in a new setting without the same prompt, we have bounded evidence of retained learning. Repetition alone would not be enough; following commands forever may leave the child no more independent.

Notice how our earlier concepts compose. A chosen boundary says whose possible action we are describing. An aperture limits what the actor can know. An account provides a provisional picture of the state. The world continues moving during the attempt. The result may differ from the plan. A better account next time can make a better choice possible, but the new state may also have removed a route that once existed.

# 4. What Travels

**Carrier** names anything that transmits an effect, resource or consequential account between places, persons or moments. Pipes carry water; documents carry statements; training and stories can carry methods; regulations transmit constraints into many later decisions. They are all carriers for a purpose of description, but they work through different mechanisms. The carrier's existence does not establish that its contents arrived, were understood, were true or were used lawfully.

**Coupling** means that a change in one part of a situation can affect, enable or constrain another part through some connection. Coupling does not mean that the parts share a single mind, common interests or equal authority. It allows us to ask how a selected action can alter another person's conditions, and how a distant consequence can return to affect the original actor.

A child carries the glass to the garden. Water arrived through the pipes; the adult taught the child how to lift it; the child waters a seed. Some water reaches the roots and some spills into a saucer. If the saucer fills, the wooden shelf can stain. The useful and unwanted effects travelled together for part of the journey, then separated. The child sees wet soil; a carpenter may notice a shelf at risk. These are different accounts of coupled consequences, not competing claims that one must erase the other.

A letter carries a promise across distance. A repair manual preserves a method an engineer may use years after its author has gone. A rumour carries an accusation faster than it can be checked. A pay record can correctly show a transfer while saying very little about the rent someone could not pay during the delay. Following carriers helps us notice where the account stops and the world continues.

Now widen the small scene. A neighbour borrows the watering can. Another person knows how to collect rainwater safely; a third notices the shared tap is costly. They do not have to become one collective person in order to make something none could maintain alone. One contributes knowledge, another a location, another time. A planter becomes a shared place. The same arrangement could fail if the work is silently imposed on the neighbour with the least time or if someone claims the right to direct everyone else simply because they bought the barrel.

Here is the first constructive composition worth keeping: a bounded person encounters another aperture, learns a usable method, tries it with feedback, retains enough skill to apply it in a different situation, and finds conditions that sustain the capability. **Carriers and practice can support formation, but do not by themselves prove learning.** We would need evidence that the person can actually perform or adapt the skill. We would also need to know who supplies the tools, pays for upkeep, bears risks and is free to withdraw. The resulting capability can be a genuine new prospect rather than merely an old menu option rediscovered.

Helping is not automatically taking control. Someone with better access may carry a necessary call, document or heavy object so that the less capable person can act on their own aim. A form of kindness is the *placement of necessary burden*: who is able and willing to carry the work, and whether putting it there keeps the other's agency intact. A capable helper is not thereby entitled to become a permanent owner of the other person's future. Consent, standing and responsibility will require later ethical discussion; the mechanics show the difference between shifting effort and merely praising care.

A small system can have multiple participants and no single chooser. A market shifts because many people buy and sell. Traffic accumulates from interacting journeys, road conditions and policies. An ecosystem changes with weather, organisms and land use. Some actors still have particular powers, duties and opportunities to intervene, even where no one person selects the entire result. We must neither invent a unified villain nor dissolve all responsibility into complexity.

We can now follow a change across boundaries. We know that an account can travel, an action can have effects through carriers, and a skill can become a sustained capability if further conditions hold. But there is still a difference between a possible future described by somebody else and a future an actual participant can reach. We need a word for the road between them.

# 5. The Road That Exists and the Road You Can Take

**Route** means a sequence of possible connections or actions leading towards some specified result. An **approachable** result is one towards which that actor can make progress. A **reachable target** is more demanding: the specified target condition can be attained by a feasible course within the declared horizon, given the actor's actual information, resources, abilities, permissions and timing. Progress towards the library door does not mean entering it; starting to learn is not the same target as retaining a skill. Where evidence is insufficient to establish the whole route, call its reachability **uncertain**, not achieved. A route on a diagram may exist without being usable for that person. We must say *whose* route, *to what* result, and *under what* conditions. These claims can be uncertain and time-bound.

There is a library across town. A map shows a road, and the building has books you would like to read. Can you go? You might need a bus that runs once an hour, a lift that is working today, money for travel, childcare, or permission to leave work. The library may close before you arrive. A correct map is not a ticket, and an advertised service is not automatically an available opportunity.

**POSSIBLE ≠ USABLE.** A published route may be real yet unavailable to the person who needs it. What they know, can afford, can reach and can finish in time determines whether the route works for them.

Sometimes no route was offered in the first place. A form may have categories for approved and rejected applications, but no category for people who never encountered the invitation, lacked the language or technology to apply, or were excluded from the target group before a file existed. **A missing record of refusal is not evidence of a fair opportunity.** The boundary of the institution's measured applicants is not necessarily the boundary of affected people. The unheard person may be invisible to the very system intended to demonstrate that access was open.

Even where a path exists in each version of the world we are considering, a person may not know how to select a safe one. Imagine two streets that look identical from the corner. One is open beyond the bend, the other blocked. In one possible arrangement the left turn succeeds; in the other, the right. An all-knowing map of both arrangements contains a successful route for each. But the traveller cannot distinguish the arrangements yet, so the evidence does not establish a **single observation-based policy guaranteed to succeed across both**. An arbitrary first turn might succeed in one. That is weaker than a warranted plan for either uncertain situation.

A sign placed before the corner can change what the traveller knows. A bridge can make a route feasible whichever street is blocked. These are not the same intervention: one improves information, the other changes the available world. Sometimes the smallest helpful thing is a better account; sometimes it is a new road; sometimes it is giving an already capable person the resources and permission to carry the work. The distinction makes positive construction more precise rather than less ambitious.

Now compose change with an account and a route. Yesterday's opening-hour notice may still be accurate, or a change in the accessible entrance may have defeated its intended present-use claim. A fresh check can update the visitor's route, provided a trustworthy observation is possible and somebody can use it. The old record is not necessarily deceptive or globally wrong; the point is whether it still warrants *this* decision. A correction route begins as an opportunity to carry newly relevant information into the place where a practical change can be made.

A **supported prospect** is a future for which a person has some credible path of development or access, with necessary conditions that can be named and examined. It differs from merely feeling hopeful. A child who wants to learn an instrument may have a teacher, an affordable instrument, a place to practise and time; the prospect is stronger than a motivational poster, though success is not guaranteed. A community workshop may create a skill nobody could acquire alone. **A supported prospect creates no duty to pursue it** or to feel optimistic, comply or endure. Where a programme calls an offer voluntary, inspect the actual price of refusal: losing essential income, housing or care can exert pressure even if that support is labelled *related* to the programme. Chapter 3's pressured-consent test applies to the person's own account, practical options and who controls those conditions. Neither a published route nor the word *voluntary* establishes free choice; nor does evidence of pressure automatically settle the legitimacy of every conditional benefit. Conditions and protective duties require separate justification, proportionate assessment and a usable way to contest their application. The value of the prospect and the legitimacy of any condition are questions of affected interests and authority, not outputs of the route description.

A road that can be used today might close tomorrow. Another road can become possible through learning, cooperation or public investment. We can now ask better questions than whether an option appears somewhere in a policy document. Is it known to the person concerned? Can they take it without surrendering something they have not agreed to give? Is it maintainable? And, once they begin, can anyone carry the action all the way to its needed effect before the relevant future closes?

The last question takes us from routes to clocks, control and power.

# 6. The Clock and the Hand on the Door

**A clock** is a way of comparing when relevant events occur or how long a selected transition takes. A clock claim needs a chosen starting point, an event that counts as completion, and some evidence about the time. **Control** here means the actual capacity to change a route, rule, resource or timing. **Exposure** means bearing the effects while those conditions continue. One person can set and experience a deadline or control a response, while in other arrangements the people who set the schedule and those who bear its consequences differ. This is a descriptive relationship, not a score for blame or entitlement.

Now return to the library across town. You know the route, can afford the bus and have permission to travel. You may still arrive too late. Knowing how to act and starting the journey are not the same as producing the protective effect in time.

Now put time back into the journey. The library may close at five. If you arrive at five-thirty, a perfectly valid ticket and a correct address will not make the visit happen. You may go tomorrow, but the book you needed for today's class cannot be borrowed in time for that class. A later visit is a different outcome.

Other paths become stronger through time. A musician practises until playing a difficult passage no longer depends on a lucky attempt. Neighbours build trust by keeping small promises. A repaired bridge is maintained so it remains usable in future storms. The same passage of time that narrows some opportunities can deepen capability and make other opportunities more dependable.

**Hardening**, in the protective timing question, means that reversing a *specified loss* becomes harder, costlier, unequal or impossible in a comparable form. A missed class, a worsening illness or a broken trust may harden at different rates. **Consolidation** is a different pattern: a practised skill, repaired bridge or kept promise becomes more dependable. A valued commitment may deliberately close options while creating stability. We should identify which path is changing; the timing of a loss does not decide whether every closure is wrong.

For an important threatened path, we may have to ask two different timing questions. When will the damage become difficult or impossible to reverse in a comparable way? And when can a particular corrective action actually be completed, given the people, permissions, resources and uncertainty involved? A fast promise is not the same as a feasible intervention.

A timely correction has several stages. Someone must **detect** the relevant change. Evidence must **reach** an actor who can consider it. The actor must **decide** and, where needed, obtain legitimate authorisation. Someone must **execute** the intervention. Finally, the intervention must actually have the **protective effect**. A form being submitted or a work order being approved is not the final effect.

Detection, reporting, authorisation and execution may overlap or need several passes. What counts is the elapsed time until the **protective effect**, supported by evidence about the particular route. A quick acknowledgement can be followed by a delay that defeats the purpose.

**Mechanical waypoint — T(protect, a) < T(harden, a).** Here **a** names the particular protective course being considered, such as repairing access or imposing a temporary hold. Both times must be elapsed from the **same starting event**, or converted using evidence that makes the starting points comparable. The first ends when that course has its required **protective effect**; the second describes when the *same threatened path under that course* becomes unrecoverable in the agreed sense. A hold can change the hardening boundary itself, so the clocks must be reassessed together. The inequality is a **necessary timing condition** for that particular prevention, not proof that the action will succeed, is authorised or is ethically justified.

The time bounds may be uncertain. An evidenced latest possible time for effective protection before an evidenced earliest relevant hardening time supports an open window **for that course**. If the needed origin, completion bounds, threatened path or effect cannot be established, the honest window status is **UNKNOWN**. Failure of one route does not prove every alternative is closed; another route or legitimate hold may preserve the path. **Closing a window for prevention of one loss does not close a different window for limiting continuing harm.** The first missed class cannot be attended yesterday, yet support today may prevent further absence, debt or worsening illness. Name the *new threatened effect*, its people and its own clock; do not disguise mitigation as restoration. Selecting which risk to carry remains a value and authority decision, not a result of clock arithmetic.

**UNKNOWN still has a clock and a bearer.** Seeking more evidence, taking a precaution or letting the current arrangement continue each changes whose risk is carried. Name who holds the information, who can investigate, who has legitimate authority to order a temporary hold, and who bears the work and risk while the question remains open. **A hold also creates an exposed party and a clock of its own:** pausing one household's work may delay another family's urgent repair, while pausing an automated refusal may require a safely supported interim payment. Track both courses, not a one-sided presumption that precaution is costless or that the two harms have equal moral weight. If no competent owner exists, that is a governance gap, not a licence to demand impossible proof. Domain law, obligations, consent and affected standing—not this diagram—determine whether investigation or interim protection is warranted.

This matters even more when a software service makes hundreds of decisions before a reviewer can examine one. Imagine that a benefits system issues an automatic refusal and offers an appeal that is manually processed after the support period expires. The system may detect a submitted appeal immediately, route it correctly and approve it eventually. Yet the effect arrives after the person has lost the threatened opportunity. Speed of the first stages is not evidence of a timely protective outcome. No invented millisecond threshold is needed to see the failure.

Different paths have different clocks. A late payment may restore money while leaving a missed train in the past. Medicine may require probabilistic estimates rather than a single deadline. When we lack sound timing bounds, **UNKNOWN** is an informative result: it tells the decision-maker where better evidence or an immediate precaution may matter.

Several open windows can also conflict. Suppose three households each need a two-day repair by one crew, with relevant losses hardening before days three, four and seven. Each job could finish within its own window **if the crew were free**. One crew cannot finish all three in time merely because each individual comparison passes. The shared crew, competing tasks and ordering must be assessed together; a second crew or a safe temporary measure may change what is feasible. If some path must wait, that priority is an accountable value and authority decision—not something a queue or the timing inequality should silently decide. Detailed scheduling belongs to competent resource planners.

Now consider who can change the journey.

The library's evening hours are set in an office that closes at four. The people choosing them have a budget, staff rotas and a building to secure. They can ask for another month's figures before deciding whether to extend the hours. For a student whose assignment is due on Friday, that month is not available. The student can explain the deadline, but cannot change the rota or keep the building open.

The office has reasons to wait. The student has reasons not to. The important difference is not that one side knows everything or that the other knows nothing. It is that they can do different things, and do not carry the same consequences while the question remains unsettled. The people who set a clock may not feel it run. The person who can describe a closed route may not be able to open it.

The path and its clocks have authors as well as observers. The institution chose a library closing time, the office chose a reporting period, and the student has a deadline set elsewhere. If a report uses the office's monthly window as though it were the student's usable time, the comparison silently privileges one position. We must say whose start point, end point and affected future the claim uses.

The same relation can appear where more is at stake. A tenant reports damp spreading behind a child's bed. The landlord can arrange an inspection and authorise repairs. The tenant can send photographs, move the bed if there is space, and try to reach someone who can act. Neither knows the full cause yet. But the child sleeps in that room while the question stays open; the person deciding when to inspect does not.

The request can be recorded as received without changing those positions. Another photograph may improve the account held by the landlord while leaving the tenant's capacity to protect the room much as it was. More information and more control are not the same change.

The library may genuinely need staff and the landlord may need specialist advice. Even so, the positions are different: one actor can commission a change while another lives with the consequence. We can describe that difference from their available actions and the passing time, without guessing anyone's hidden motives.

The same position can carry help. A pharmacist notices a possible problem with a prescription. Instead of asking the patient to carry the query between offices, she contacts the prescriber and keeps the patient informed. Her access and working relationship make a route available that the patient could not readily use alone. The difficulty has moved toward someone with the means to carry it.

**Mechanical waypoint — CONTROL, EXPOSURE AND BENEFIT.** Ask three separate questions: **who can alter the route; who lives with what happens while waiting; and who benefits from the route staying as it is?** These positions may coincide or differ. The landlord may gain time or rent while the tenant lives with damp; an incentive can preserve an unusable route even without evidence of malice. A beneficiary of claimed necessity or closure should not be its only unchecked judge. The pharmacist shows the constructive counterpart: someone with access can carry the difficulty and open a usable route. None of these positions alone establishes blame, entitlement or legal duty.

# 7. When Change Matters to Someone

**Value** names what is treated as worth protecting, enabling or avoiding in an ethical comparison; **standing** identifies whose interests or testimony may properly count; **authority** concerns who may legitimately decide or intervene and under which rules. These are commitments and institutions to be stated, defended and contested, not consequences that follow automatically from a boundary, a model or technical capability. In each consequential judgement ask who selected the value, scope, comparison baseline and horizon, how that person acquired decision authority, and who has a route to challenge the selection. Transparency about the selection does not by itself make the selection just.

Standing itself may be **unknown or disputed**. Leaving it unresolved in an account is different from allowing whoever benefits from exclusion to treat the affected entity as having no interests by default. The content of a worthwhile life belongs, where applicable, to those living it—not to the person offering a new route. Legitimate representation, protection and any priority between conflicting claims still require the relevant law, evidence and ethical argument.

Now return to the library door. It is closed when you arrive. Your ticket is valid, and you cannot enter. Those facts describe an outcome. Do they show you have been wronged?

Perhaps you arrived after the published hours, or the building was closed for an emergency. Or perhaps a policy excluded a person who was entitled to enter. The same locked door can play different roles in an ethical judgement. We need to know whose interests and obligations are at stake.

Calling that outcome a harm requires us to apply the commitments just named to the situation. The route may have closed to protect someone else from a fire, or the institution may have failed an access duty. Mechanics exposes the difference and the burden; competent law, community arrangements, affected voices and reasoned moral argument determine how the commitments apply.

The judgement now needs declared inputs: **who is affected; which interests count; who chose the boundary, comparison and time horizon; who has standing and authority; and how can a contested claim be answered?** A decision-maker may disclose all these and still be mistaken or unjust. Disclosure makes the disagreement inspectable; it does not settle it.

Consider the library board. It may legally set opening hours, but lawful competence does not guarantee good judgement. A student can be strongly affected without acquiring sole authority over the budget; a manager can control the budget without having first-hand knowledge of an inaccessible door. Relevant law, rights, democratic arrangements, professional expertise and affected people's accounts may disagree. Mechanical Ethics cannot supply a universal priority order by renaming these differences. It can keep them in view for the people authorised and answerable to decide.

**DESCRIPTION ≠ ETHICAL JUDGEMENT; CAPABILITY ≠ AUTHORITY.** The door being closed is a fact to investigate. Whether closing it was justified also depends on interests, duties, evidence and the legitimate decision process. The person technically able to control a door may not be the person entitled to decide its use.

Consider a child about to run into a road. An adult closes the garden gate. The child protests because the garden path is no longer open. The adult has curtailed one possibility and may have protected several more. To judge the act we need more than a count of closed options. We need an account of the child, the risk, the adult's responsibilities, the other people who may be affected, and the kind of future the intervention is meant to protect.

There may be disagreement about the proper boundary. At what age should the child be allowed to open the gate alone? What supervision is reasonable? Whose judgement matters, and when does the child's growing independence change the answer? These are questions of standing, care, evidence, responsibility and social authority. They do not resolve themselves from the fact that time passes.

A similar problem arises with non-human life. The tree beside the library needs water and space. Its roots may damage a wall. The people maintaining the building have responsibilities, and the tree belongs to an ecosystem not represented by one human voice. Which lives and futures count? Who may represent them? The answers may come from law, ecological knowledge, community judgement and ethical disagreement. Calling all things entities does not quietly settle their standing.

Mechanical Ethics makes its guiding commitment openly: consequential harm should be easier to see before it hardens, affected people should have reachable means of correction, and worthwhile capabilities should be possible to build without hiding their costs. Readers may contest how this commitment applies and which institutions may act on it.

A reader may accept much of that commitment and disagree about its application. Two people may care about justice and differ over what a fair outcome requires. A worker's privacy can conflict with an employer's duty to investigate misconduct. A town may want both a flood defence and a living river. Parents and young people may disagree over safety and independence. A method that turns such disagreement into a single unquestionable number would conceal what ought to be argued about.

We can do better than saying that values are merely subjective noise. We can ask where a judgement came from. Is it the affected person's expressed preference? A legal obligation? A clinical judgement? A community decision? A provisional assumption made because the true preference is unavailable? Has someone with the relevant standing been excluded? A value may be well supported or deeply contested. Those differences can remain visible.

The child eventually learns to open the gate safely. That learning may also change the child’s standing in the next decision. A protective arrangement that was justified yesterday must be able to recognize a newly usable independent route; otherwise care hardens into control. The earlier protection did not have to last in the same form. An arrangement that treats yesterday's vulnerability as permanent may become a barrier to tomorrow's capability.

The child's growing skill shows why values and circumstances must be reconsidered together. Protection that was useful last year may become an obstacle to independence this year. We next need a disciplined way to compare possible arrangements without concealing whose interests are included.

# 8. Comparing Without Hiding the Difference

**Comparison** is a claim about two or more described courses under a declared basis. Before announcing a harm difference, identify the affected scope, the proposed action, its feasible comparator, the period, the sources of evidence and the values through which outcomes are interpreted. A numerical subtraction is defensible only if its quantities, units and aggregation choices have actually been justified. This language does not create a universal measure of dignity, wellbeing or deservingness.

Imagine the library considers extending its evening hours.

A longer opening could help students, shift workers and families who cannot visit during the day. It could also cost more in wages and energy, lengthen the journey home for staff and reduce resources for services that some other visitors need. An early trial might attract many readers. It might also reveal that people who most needed evening access still cannot use the last bus.

We need a comparison. But comparison with what?

Begin with the baseline: compare longer library hours with which real alternative—the current timetable, a smaller extension, or another use of the budget? Then identify the scope: students, staff, carers, neighbours and anyone else whose interests enter the decision. Finally choose the horizon: the trial evenings, the term or consequences that take longer to appear. A claim about “less harm” changes meaning if any of these are silently replaced.

The comparison itself has authors. A formal panel may choose which outcomes enter the report, a funder may select the time horizon, and an affected worker may contest both. An honest ledger records those decisions, their authority and any people the selected boundary excludes. The person who chooses that comparison basis is part of the story. A council could compare a new crossing with no crossing, or with a cheaper repair to the existing route. A user may care about the day the crossing will open, while the council's report spans a financial year. We should preserve the author and reason for each selection instead of making it appear that “the numbers” fixed the boundary. This is compatible with rigorous measurement; it is a condition for interpreting what measurement means.

**SAME CLAIM, DECLARED COMPARISON BASIS.** Name the comparator, affected people, period, evidence and value convention. If unlike outcomes cannot defensibly be measured on one scale, keep their differences visible instead of inventing a total.

A number can still clarify a narrowly measured part. If one library actually opens for two hours a week and a new timetable offers four, the change in *published opening time* for that library and week is `4 - 2 = 2 hours`. This does not establish two hours of usable access for every visitor, much less two hours of moral benefit; buses, work, accessibility and staffing remain separate claims. The comparison becomes misleading if we silently change the library, period or quantity being measured.

One possibility is to compare the new hours with the current timetable. Another is to compare them with a different allocation of the same budget. A third is to compare them with a future in which the building closes entirely because its running costs are unsustainable. The proposal can look different under each reference. Choosing a baseline is part of the claim, not a neutral clerical step.

The period also matters. A one-week trial may create an immediate gain and hide an unsustainable staffing burden. A five-year plan may promise eventual savings while demanding more than current staff can safely carry. The same project can have a positive long-run ambition and an unjustified short-run transition. A good destination does not erase the journey people have to live through.

We must name the affected scope. Are we talking about one student, every evening visitor, the employees, the neighbourhood, or future taxpayers? Expanding the boundary can reveal previously hidden effects. It can also dissolve a person's serious injury into a comfortable average. A large total benefit does not, by itself, tell us what may permissibly be imposed on a smaller group.

Then there is the difficulty of comparing unlike things. An hour of paid labour, an hour of quiet study, a person's dignity, a wetland's health and an institution's cash reserve are not interchangeable units merely because they appear in the same report. A numerical total requires an authored way of valuing and combining them. If someone supplies that way, we should know who it was and what it excludes.

We can speak of a harm difference between two described paths: how much worse or better an outcome is, for some affected scope, under a stated account of value, over a stated period, compared with a stated alternative.

Suppose the extended library hours are a success for visitors but a loss for two staff members whose childcare arrangements no longer work. Their burden cannot be cancelled invisibly by the phrase community benefit. It may be possible to redesign the rota, fund transport, recruit willing evening staff or shorten the extension. The point of bringing the difference into view is to make more constructive choices possible.

Sometimes two legitimate values still collide. A library may have to balance quiet study against a noisy community event. The same space and hour cannot serve both perfectly. A city may have to decide between spending on immediate accessibility and a longer-term flood project. The technical facts can narrow the plausible alternatives; they cannot choose the political and moral priority without some legitimate decision process.

A fair process may itself be contested. Who was invited? Who could attend? Were private reasons exposed unnecessarily? Could someone disagree without losing access to a service? Did those most affected have a voice with any consequence? None of this guarantees consensus. It helps distinguish disagreement made answerable from a verdict hidden inside the method of counting.

The library opens two evenings on a trial basis, with paid volunteers from the staff team, an accessible transport arrangement and a clear date for reconsideration. Some visitors gain new possibilities. The costs are not erased. Evidence will have to show what actually happened, including what did not work.

# 9. What Can Travel Back

**Witness** means an account, person, instrument or record whose means of knowing can support a particular later claim. **Correction route** means an evidenced path by which consequential information can reach someone able and authorised to change a relevant outcome, including an effect that can be checked. A record can be true and still lack such a route. Independence concerns how the witness actually learned and kept information, not how many file copies exist.

The first library evening goes well. The second is quiet. A manager receives a report saying thirty people attended over two nights.

The number may be accurate. It does not tell us whether the students who were expected to benefit arrived, why someone left early, whether the last bus was reachable, or what the staff experienced. It may reveal a useful pattern while leaving out the reason the trial was begun.

A visitor writes a note: the building was open, but the accessible entrance had already been locked. A staff member reports that the proposed closing time conflicts with a scheduled bus. A student says the extra hour made it possible to finish an assignment. These are not the same kind of testimony. Each is limited, and each can matter.

Suppose the visitor photographs the locked entrance. The library's door system logs the time it was secured. A third person remembers seeing someone unable to enter. If the photograph and log were retained independently before anyone knew there would be a dispute, they may support a stronger reconstruction than either alone. Even then, the evidence needs interpretation. The door could have been locked for an emergency. The photograph may not show another available entrance. The visitor might have been unable to use that other entrance. The competing accounts need to be allowed to meet.

Now ask whether a purported check was fit for this dispute. A rule requiring door checks might exist without anyone having **performed** one that evening. A check might have run but inspected only the main entrance, never the accessible side door. Even three genuinely independent records that could not observe that door cannot establish its accessibility. **CHECK EXISTS ≠ CHECK RAN ≠ CHECK COULD DETECT THIS FAILURE.** A credible conclusion needs evidence of execution and of a check capable of distinguishing the failure that matters; a check result alone is not a repair.

**Custody** is the practical history of who held a record, who controlled its copies and clocks, and what might have changed. A timestamp records what a particular clock showed; a checksum can support evidence that particular bytes remained unchanged. Neither proves the event's truth, and three files repeating one witness are not three independent accounts.

Custody matters because records can be altered, deleted, exposed or used against the person who created them. A person in a precarious job may have strong evidence of a dangerous practice and good reasons not to place it in a manager's public folder. A person seeking medical care may need a confidential channel rather than a public accusation. A child cannot be made responsible for building an evidence archive about a situation adults should address.

It helps to distinguish access from authority to disclose. The library may possess security recordings without having the right to publish every visitor's movements. A file may be readable on an open website because somebody uploaded it, while its presence there says nothing about lawful or ethical reuse. Privacy is not dishonesty. Withholding a fact does not make the fact false or absent. A witness can support correction without becoming an excuse for universal surveillance.

If the library changes the closing procedure, it should preserve enough of the reason for later staff to understand it. But it may not need to preserve the student's full identity or the visitor's unrelated movements. Responsible memory is purpose-bound, not a hunger for every available detail.

An adversarial arrangement may have a complaint link but make it dangerous to use. A worker who has seen retaliation may reasonably refuse to send a signed report. An institution may withhold the only log or alter its clock; it may demand a category the affected person was never offered. Those are describable route conditions, though they do not prove an individual decision-maker's inner motive. Protecting a private witness, finding a lawful independent receiver or admitting there is no safe effective path may be more truthful than counting the feedback form as an accomplished correction.

The manager answers, 'We have taken this seriously.' It may be sincere, but the accessible door remains locked. The decisive question is whether the information can reach someone entitled and able to change the process.

**Mechanical waypoint — RECORD ≠ REMEDY; FEEDBACK ≠ ANSWERABILITY.** *Answerability* means a concern has a **sufficiently safe and usable** route to a responsible receiver who can arrange proportionate consideration; material evidence and competing reasons can be examined; and the result, including a legitimate reasoned refusal, can be challenged through a usable route. Receipt, written reasons or a formally available appeal alone are insufficient if the process never engages the actual concern or cannot be used without unreasonable danger. Whether a safe route exists may itself be **UNKNOWN**. Fair consideration does not guarantee a correct outcome or mean every request must be granted.

**Triage** means accountable allocation of finite review capacity. Urgency, plausible risk, available evidence, relevant duties and who can obtain missing information should guide preliminary assessment, investigation, referral, immediate protection or reasoned closure. An affected person may be unable to obtain records controlled by the institution; that gap is not itself proof the claim lacks merit. As in Chapter 6, ask who controls the evidence and who carries the risk while uncertainty persists. Categories, priorities and refusals require proportionate avenues of challenge; there is no unlimited entitlement to investigation, but an unsupported rubber stamp is not an answer. Where correction is warranted, information must reach someone authorised and able to produce a checkable change. Confidentiality and the person's right to stop remain material constraints.

There must be some route from the affected information to a person or mechanism able to alter what happens. A clerk may be allowed to read the complaint but not to change building access. A senior manager may have the authority and no access to the original evidence. A provider may run the software while the library owns the policy. The strength of the witness alone cannot repair the connection.

Some correction routes fail because the parts are disconnected. Others are kept unusable by design or incentive. An appeal may require the applicant to repeat the same evidence through a channel that loses attachments. A contractor may be paid for closed tickets rather than restored access. A manager may receive every warning yet face a stronger reward for leaving a decision unchanged. A complaint procedure can technically exist while surveillance, retaliation, expense or exhaustion makes its use dangerous.

That is an observable arrangement, not proof of a secret malicious intention. We can describe the requirements, response times, lost messages, incentives and harms without pretending to read anyone's mind. If evidence supports deliberate obstruction, it can be named as a further claim with its own witnesses. If a person with power reads the evidence and declines to act, refusal alone does not decide the test: was the concern considered fairly, were genuine reasons given, were applicable duties met, and is there a safe, usable challenge where warranted? A justified refusal can be answerable. A rubber-stamped dismissal or an unchallengeable refusal that leaves an established duty unmet is not. Feedback reaching an inbox proves neither.

Imagine a public-service appeal in which a claimant can submit a letter but can never see whether it was received, cannot correct the record used against them, and loses eligibility before any answer is issued. Sending more letters does not establish an effective remedy. A trustworthy description must be capable of saying: **the route was blocked; no effective owner or safe alternative is currently known.** It must also recognise that the affected person may choose not to expose themselves to further retaliation. Describing the obstruction is useful; it cannot force an institution to put matters right.

Nor can an affected person's voice always be assumed to speak for every other affected person. The quiet reader and the music group may disagree about evening use. A worker can challenge an unsafe rota without having authority to decide the entire staffing budget. Legitimate standing and decision power need to be distinguished rather than granted to whoever most convincingly describes a harm.

The library adjusts its entrance procedure, keeps a telephone route for people unable to use its app, and appoints a staff member who can actually authorise changes to the closing process. The first two evenings have become a basis for better work. No one had to know the entire world to achieve it. Several partial accounts were able to correct a limited part of it.

Repair needs to survive the original complaint. The changed entrance procedure must reach the next shift, stay usable, and have a responsible owner. An individual case can be closed while the same mechanism still harms the next visitor; those are different outcomes.

The value of the record lies not in how impressive it looks but in what truthful, proportionate change it makes possible.

# 10. What Cannot Be Put Back

**Correction** is a supported change to a relevant process, decision or effect in response to new information. **Restoration** means re-establishing a particular lost condition or path in a comparable form. **Residue** is what remains uncorrected or cannot be restored by the described intervention. The three are different claims requiring different evidence; a database flag marked resolved does not settle any of them.

A student writes to the library after its trial. The extended opening hours were useful, the student says, but the locked entrance on the first night meant missing the last session of a course. The library has now fixed the door procedure. The student is pleased that somebody listened and that others will not face the same barrier. The missed session will not return.

The repaired entrance helps future visitors and may support a remedy for the first student. The missed lesson is still past. A useful account can recognise the improvement and the remaining loss at the same time.

Present life matters as well as possible future options. Someone facing another exhausting treatment, for example, may value comfort and relationships now more than a poorly supported promise of extra interventions. The decision still belongs to the person and competent care arrangements; counting additional options cannot settle their worth.

**Mechanical waypoint — CORRECTED ≠ RESTORED.** “The entrance now works” and “the student received the missed lesson” are different factual claims. One can be true while the other remains false. Later support can matter without pretending an earlier loss has been erased.

The same reasoning applies to development that never happened. A student who could not attend a course may lose not only those sessions but the skill, relationship or confidence that might have grown from them. That possible future is difficult to measure and should not be invented as a certainty. Still, excluding all unrealised formation from the account can hide a serious consequence. The honest comparison names the lost opportunity, its uncertainty, and whether any later route could support similar learning.

An account can be reopened while the debt accumulated during its closure remains. A job can be reinstated while reputation, wages and trust have changed. A wall can be repaired after a child has spent a winter in a damp room. A rule can be withdrawn after people have left a community. The later improvement is real. So may be the remaining cost.

Call a consequence left beyond the reach of a particular correction its **residue**. Reopening a file may not return lost wages, trust or learning time. The question is which effects a remedy actually changes and which require further attention.

Some effects are irreversible in a narrow physical sense. The past event cannot be made never to have happened. That does not prove that continuing harm remains forever; people can heal, relationships can change and new futures can be made. Some harms are gradually repairable, some are compensable without being reversible, and some leave an enduring loss. A serious account must let these differences stand.

Sometimes the most responsible act is to stop an intervention rather than continue it in the hope that another stage will justify the first. A medical procedure, an engineering experiment or an AI-assisted decision may have reached a point where evidence or authority is insufficient for the next move. A pause can preserve possibilities; a pause can also have costs. Whether it is warranted depends on the affected route, the clocks, the expertise and the authority involved.

Sometimes there is no effective rescue. The decision-maker is distant, the institution refuses to listen, or the relevant time has passed. It would be cruel to tell the person harmed that a better record will certainly deliver justice. Evidence can help an appeal, protect someone else or preserve an honest history. It can also fail. The witness should not be made to carry responsibility for a remedy that the powerful actor has withheld.

What remains, then, of answerability when the original loss cannot be corrected?

The people involved may still be owed an accurate account, a lawful remedy, a change to the repeated mechanism, protection against further misuse, or an honest explanation of what cannot be repaired. Which of these is owed depends on the actual responsibilities and values at stake. An institution cannot make the past good by rewriting its status field. Nor can it promise complete restitution where no one can supply it.

The person harmed may wish to stop talking. That is not proof that the matter was trivial, but neither is continued testimony always something others may demand. Privacy, rest and refusal can be ways of preserving what remains open. Care does not require possession of another person's future; it may require placing the work of correction on those with the resources and authority to carry it.

Closure itself needs judgement. Some disputes should end because continued proceedings impose more harm than they can remedy, because an affected party has chosen a legitimate exit, or because the evidence is insufficient to justify coercive action. Other files are closed too early to protect the institution from embarrassment. No single timer or form can settle the difference for every domain.

Return to the child and the glass. A broken glass cannot be made unbroken by calling a replacement the same object. A new glass may serve the child just as well, and the child may have learned to carry it. The earlier break still occurred. No ethical theory needs to make the break into an eternal injury or pretend it never happened. Honest repair can be both limited and worthwhile.

The student accepts the library's apology and decides not to pursue a further complaint. The library still changes its procedure and pays attention to the next student. Their decisions are different, and both matter.

**Peace is not proof of repair.** Accepting compensation, forgiving somebody, or becoming too exhausted to continue may be a real personal choice; none establishes that yesterday's missed lesson was restored or tomorrow's entrance will work. The student's right to stop is not a duty to keep supplying testimony until an institution can mark itself repaired.

The next student's route is part of the repair claim, not a sentimental epilogue. Staff need to know the changed instruction, the usable entry and the person who can repair a fresh failure. If no one can keep it working, the local apology may be genuine while the harmful mechanism remains. A witness may choose silence or closure without becoming responsible for maintaining the institution's correction.

# 11. The Things We Make Possible

**Formation** names the development of a capability, habit, relationship or arrangement over time. A new capability is not established just because someone attended a session or followed a prompt. We need some evidence of usable performance, retention or transfer under stated conditions. **Maintenance** names the work and resources that keep a capability or route available; it may reveal hidden burdens on the people doing that work.

The child returns to the pot on the windowsill.

A leaf has opened. The child has learned that watering matters, that too much water has consequences, and that the plant changes on its own timetable. An adult has learnt to put a tray beneath the pot instead of blaming the child for a shelf that cannot bear a spill. Somebody has taught the child what to notice, and the child has begun to notice without being told.

The child laughs at the ridiculous lean of a sunflower, paints the pot in clashing colours and watches an insect with a friend. Such moments need not be qualifications for later work. **Play, curiosity and companionship have value in the present**, alongside skills that might last. They still need room, time and people who can keep the conditions available.

Formation has carriers and clocks as real as the correction of injury. A person needs occasions to practise, feedback on mistakes, tools they can reach and enough continuity for yesterday's learning to affect tomorrow's attempt. A workshop open only once before its equipment disappears may inspire somebody but fail to sustain a new skill. Regular access, patient teaching and safe correction can build one. The person learning still has agency: help must leave room to refuse, change direction or surpass the teacher.

This is a constructive composition of the earlier grammar. An aperture lets the child notice the plant's condition; a trusted adult carries an explanation; an action changes the watering; feedback revises the account; practice under tolerable conditions may develop a skill; and maintenance keeps the materials available. If the child can later care for a different plant without being led through every step, there is stronger evidence of transfer. None of the earlier components alone guaranteed that result. A coerced practice schedule that never produces independent performance is a counterexample, not a hidden success.

It is worth seeing the difference between protecting an existing opportunity and making a new one. The child could always stand by the window. They could not always care for a plant. The skill is not a new object that can be handed over in one motion. It has been formed through repeated encounters, trial, correction, encouragement and conditions that allowed mistakes without unnecessary punishment.

Something similar happens when people learn a language, repair a bicycle, recover confidence after illness, become able to trust a friend, or discover a community that has room for them. Their future is not merely a list of options that were stored somewhere waiting to be unlocked. Other people, institutions, materials and histories help create what they can become able to do.

People may need protection from immediate injury and room to learn, create, work, play, care and explore. A good arrangement makes those pursuits possible in practice: through usable places, dependable help, time, equipment and freedom to choose. Its upkeep must also be counted.

The library's evening hours are a modest example. A student gains access to a quiet room. Over several weeks, that room becomes somewhere they can study without being interrupted. A volunteer teaches a child how to search for reliable information. A new friendship begins. These are not all consequences that the library can predict, own or claim as its performance. They are possibilities supported by an arrangement whose users may make of it more than its builders imagined.

The arrangement has to be sustained. Electricity and maintenance cost money. Staff need rest. Someone must replace books and repair the lift. The students' gains are not free just because entry to the building is free. A claimed benefit that depends indefinitely on unacknowledged or coerced labour is not an honest description of success.

Making a route available once differs from making it maintainable.

Ask who actually bears the maintenance. A library's longer hours may depend on cleaners, carers, volunteer organisers, technicians and night transport that do not appear in the celebratory attendance figure. If their time, consent or compensation is missing, the institution may be shifting the cost of one group's new capability onto people with less power. Maintenance can also be an opening: training a second repairer and handing over a clear guide makes the useful arrangement less fragile. We should describe both the capacity gained and the dependencies that keep it alive. A training programme may produce a skill that decays without practice. A friendship may need attention. A forest restoration project requires care through seasons when the first volunteers have moved away. The future contains the work of upkeep, and the people who will inherit that work deserve a place in the decision.

A constructive decision therefore has its own window of opportunity. A young person who needs a placement this term cannot necessarily use a programme promised five years later. An ecological restoration can miss a breeding season and still provide gains later. In each case, the target, the starting conditions, the time to become effective and the costs of sustaining the gain are part of the proposal. We should not evaluate creation solely by counting imagined future options.

What matters is the capability being formed, the people able to use it, and the conditions that allow it to last. A useful skill can also be misused, so the judgement of its value belongs with the openly stated purposes and affected people introduced earlier.

That question can protect independence without requiring isolation. A student becomes more independent because teachers, public services, tools and other learners were present. A disabled person may gain autonomy through dependable assistance rather than through abandonment. A skilled engineer may depend on colleagues who are free to challenge a design. Mutual dependence is not inherently a failure of agency. Dependence becomes dangerous when one party can withdraw essential help without answer, conceal costs or make refusal impossible.

Care must therefore be distinguished from control. A parent helps a child cross a road and, as the child grows, learns when to let the child cross independently. A carer may know what a patient usually wants and still need to ask what the patient wants today. An AI assistant may provide valuable explanations while its user retains ways to question, learn without it or seek a human expert. The changing needs of the relationship matter more than preserving a fixed image of the helper as always necessary.

**Shared capability** is a usable result created through joined contributions that no participant could currently provide alone. It exists through the joins: skills, room, tools, time, permissions and continuing maintenance. The strength of such a capability depends on how those contributions remain connected.

The shared planter from the beginning can widen into something none of its neighbours could have made alone. A mechanic has repair skills but no suitable room. The library can lend a room but has nobody able to test an electrical appliance. The residents' group knows which households need assistance, and another person can arrange safe insurance and the tools. Put together, these separate holdings might support a monthly repair workshop. A chair and a broken toaster are easier to bring there than a life story. A volunteer may help someone repair their own bicycle rather than taking it away and returning it mysteriously repaired.

The workshop also shows a limit worth noticing. If the organiser alone carries every unpaid Saturday and has no safe way to refuse, the arrangement may exhaust the person sustaining it. The group can respond by training another organiser, sharing costs and making exit possible. A new collective capability needs maintenance and consent as well as enthusiasm.

Imagine a town that decides to build a new public workshop, a greener street and a reliable path to the library. It might be tempted to proclaim an ideal future and silence anybody who points to a cost. It would be wiser to see imagination as the horizon that opens inquiry. The promise is not the proof. Who can reach the new place? Who will maintain it? Which other worthwhile use of the land or money will be lost? Who may leave or disagree? What evidence would make the next design better?

The child has not learned a philosophical system. They have learnt to care for a plant and to notice that care changes both the plant and the carer. That is enough to continue from the windowsill toward a world larger than the room.

# 12. When Nobody Holds the Whole Wheel

**Distributed causation** means that an outcome emerges from interacting contributions without a single chooser determining all of it. Keep five questions distinct: what helped cause the outcome; who could intervene; whose duty was engaged; whether evidence supports culpability; and what remedy is realistically available. These may point to different actors. No proportional guilt equation follows from the causal map. Where no actor has adequate power to repair the route, governance itself may need redesign.

On a busy morning, a thousand people set out for work.

They choose different routes. A delivery vehicle breaks down. A hospital shift changes. Rain falls sooner than forecast. Traffic thickens. A lane closes because a utility crew finds a damaged cable. The resulting delay affects people who never made a decision about that cable.

Who chose the traffic jam?

In ordinary speech we say the city did this or the system failed. Those phrases can help us locate a problem, but they can also create the illusion of a single mind making one decision. Much of the outcome is produced by interacting processes: physical constraints, individual choices, regulations, business incentives, chance and decisions made at several levels of authority.

A description that demands one identifiable chooser for every consequence will leave much of this reality unexplained.

There may still be accountable actors. A road authority may have responsibility for signs. A contractor may have failed to secure a site. A transport agency may have chosen a schedule knowing it would leave no accessible connection. Responsibility should be traced to actual capacity, duty and control, not invented for a convenient villain or dissolved into the word system whenever a powerful actor would rather disappear.

Several questions are easily collapsed into the accusation “who caused it?” A driver may have made a **causal contribution**. A road authority may have the **capacity to intervene**. A contractor may carry an **assigned duty**. Determining **culpability** may require evidence of knowledge, competence, foreseeability and law. A **remedy** may belong to yet another actor or fund. These positions sometimes overlap and sometimes do not. There is no responsible general equation that divides moral blame in proportion to estimated causal contribution.

A distributed failure may have no single actor with enough power to repair it. That should not be disguised by sending the injured person to five departments in a circle. It may show a governance gap: authority, resources and a safe complaint route need to be brought together or assigned to an accountable body. Which institution may do that is a matter for law, policy, democratic legitimacy and domain expertise, not an automatic instruction issued by this vocabulary.

A market presents the same difficulty. Prices move because many participants buy, sell, wait and respond to information. Some firms and regulators can strongly affect the outcome, while no single actor chooses it in full. A family may lose housing through a chain of interest rates, wages, landlord decisions, planning constraints and law. An adequate account keeps the interacting causes visible and asks what each responsible actor could realistically have done.

The natural world is broader still. A river is changed by rainfall, soil, plants, farming, temperature and human construction. There may be no chooser at all for part of the process. Yet decisions about a dam, pollution or flood protection can alter the reachable futures of households and habitats for decades. People not yet born will inherit some of those changes and cannot attend the meeting that authorises them.

Here the language of bounded views and carried effects remains useful, but standing and representation grow more difficult. A future generation cannot give current consent. A woodland does not file a complaint. People may reasonably disagree about how to give their interests weight. Ecological science, public law, indigenous stewardship, democratic institutions and moral deliberation have methods we should not replace with the word entity. The question is how to keep these diverse forms of knowledge and obligation accessible within a common account of consequential change.

Now consider a computer system that sorts the thousand morning journeys into suggested routes.

It may reduce traffic and save time. Its model may not include every road closure, disability or safety concern. A driver may follow a suggested shortcut through a narrow residential street. A policy designed to optimise average journey time can impose noise and danger on residents who are not using the app and may have no way to challenge it.

The software does not need an inner wish to inconvenience them. It acts through data, objectives, infrastructure and human reliance. The people who designed it, deployed it, set permissions and retained logs may have different knowledge and different authority. Attributing everything to "the AI" can hide the people and institutions who decide what it is allowed to change.

Machine speed alters the stakes. A sensor, questionnaire or visible monitoring system can also change behaviour being measured; records are not passive windows in every setting. Asking people for more evidence may help reveal a fault, or create a surveillance risk and encourage hiding. At machine speed, a dubious classification can propagate before a human review can act. A bounded hold needs a defined affected scope, a route to actual stop authority and an understood recovery path; an instantaneous button on a dashboard proves none of these. The same system can also make possible improvements that manual work could never deliver. The interesting question is not whether the machine is good or bad in general, but whether this particular arrangement carries benefits and burdens honestly, maintains a usable way to correct its behaviour and preserves the authority of those entitled to decide.

A person advertised as human in the loop may be asked to approve too many outputs to understand any of them. That may be a ceremonial witness, not meaningful oversight. A reviewer who checked ten ordinary outputs may have **run** a check without being able to detect the unusual failure now causing harm. Another person who can inspect representative and relevant evidence, test a suspected error, and effectively halt the affected class of decisions may provide stronger answerability with fewer clicks. The question is what this particular review could detect and change, not how many approvals were recorded.

Even an emergency stop is not magic. It has a scope, a response time and consequences of its own. Stopping a traffic system may send every driver toward a dangerous alternative. A good stop needs an accountable owner and an understood recovery path. A route is not usable because somebody drew a button on the screen.

At this scale, care becomes a design question as well as a personal one. Does an arrangement let its beneficiaries inspect important advice, decline participation where legitimate, preserve their knowledge if the service changes and reach accountable people when their futures are at stake? Does it unfairly shift costs to less visible entities, including workers, neighbours, non-users and the environment?

An ambitious system can be a genuine gift. It can widen knowledge, create useful work, protect people and help communities coordinate. That possibility deserves exploration. A gift becomes less valuable when accepting it requires surrendering the ability to question what it does.

**RECURRING PATTERN ≠ IDENTICAL MECHANISM.** A transport network and an AI service can both have invisible costs and weak correction routes, but their causes and obligations must be investigated using the relevant expertise.

# 13. The Shape That Returns

**Cross-domain transfer** means reusing a carefully stated question or relation while rechecking the actual mechanism and authority in the new setting. A recurring shape is not proof of identical causation, equal standing or a scale-invariant moral law. Good transfer asks what a knowledgeable owner in the new domain must add—and knows when to stop translating.

The child at the windowsill looks down at the seed.

At first there was simply a pot, some soil, a glass and a change already under way. Then the child learnt to notice what could be seen and what lay outside that view. They acted, waited, changed their mind and sometimes misjudged the result. Other people supplied water, knowledge, time and materials. Some routes to growth became easier, others harder. They cared about the seed and needed to learn what its care required.

In a household, somebody may know a problem first while somebody else can fix it. A promise travels between people. Delay can make an apology less useful, and an improved habit can make trust grow. In a school, teachers, children and families have different knowledge and authority; a record may carry a warning, a grade or a useful observation, but an education cannot be reduced to those records. In a hospital, diagnosis, consent, evidence, risk and time are governed by professional responsibilities much stronger than those surrounding an ordinary household dispute.

In an engineering firm, an early warning may need to travel from a technician to a person with stop authority before a design is deployed. Formal tests, independent review and specialist standards matter. In a city, laws and budgets determine who can repair a crossing or create a new public service. In an AI system, data and code carry choices at speed, often to people who never agreed to participate. In a landscape, irreversible land use and slow ecological processes can change the conditions available to beings generations apart.

A sick patient cannot be treated like a software ticket. The language may help locate a warning, deadline or contested record, but the treatment decision needs clinical competence, consent and medical duties. The same questions open inquiry; they cannot transfer professional authority.

An engineer receiving a fault report may legitimately halt a software deployment under a defined stop rule. A clinician faced with a deteriorating patient must work under different evidence standards, consent rules and immediate duties of care. Copying the engineer's threshold into medicine would be an unsafe analogy. At that boundary, this portable language must hand over to the stronger domain method.

Perhaps we can call this a middle-out way of looking. It begins with something graspable: a change, a being, a choice, a relationship. It follows the necessary connections outward and inward. It does not insist that a complete theory must descend from above before an ordinary decision can be made, nor that the world's difficulties can be understood by looking only at tiny pieces in isolation.

If our language tells us that "nothing was heard", we should ask what was expected to arrive, through which channel and by when. Silence is meaningful relative to an expected signal or duty; otherwise absence can be invented from a blank field. If we say two events corroborate one another, we must ask whether they arose independently or copied the same source. If we say an intervention prevented a harm, we need a defensible comparison with what might otherwise have happened, not only a pleasing result after the event.

An unfamiliar system can sometimes be understood more quickly by asking what it can see, how it changes, who else is affected, which routes remain practical and who can revise its actions. That may reveal a hidden cost, an inaccessible appeal or a promising opportunity. At other times the correct response is to use established expertise and stop translating. Mechanics of water belong to hydrology. Diagnoses belong to competent medicine. Duties and entitlement belong to law and legitimate authority. A language that refuses to hand work back to its stronger owners becomes another dangerous bureaucracy.

Across these settings, the repeated questions help locate what to investigate; the answers must still be earned in the setting itself. We can now carry the whole language back into one ordinary room and see what it lets us notice.

# 14. The Room Again

The meanings have begun to work together. A changing world can be seen through several limited views. Those views become accounts; accounts can travel, influence choices and sometimes be corrected. An action can open a route or close it, and time determines which effects still reach the people concerned. Where an effect matters, power and responsibility enter the picture. Where a person learns or several people cooperate, the same connections may produce new capability. Hold that sequence lightly. Each connection still needs evidence, and the people affected must be able to answer back.

Come back to the glass on the table.

The water has warmed. The light has moved. Somebody may have drunk from it or left it untouched. The seed on the sill is larger, or the soil has dried, or the pot has broken. The same room holds several futures, not all of them chosen by the person who first looked around.

The facts in the room matter differently to different people. Choosing what counts as harm or benefit also involves duties, values, representation and a legitimate process for disagreement. The descriptive language helps make those choices visible so they can be answered for.

Imagine the library's inaccessible door one last time. The affected student has a limited view of the reason it was locked; the door's status and the student's deadline continue to change. The closing rule travels through a physical lock, staff practice and a schedule. The student can report the problem but cannot alter the lock. The report needs a safe evidential route, a receiver with relevant authority, an executed change and an effect that occurs before the next student loses access. We must then say what earlier loss was left behind and what new opportunities can be maintained. These are not separate slogans. Each later question depends on the preceding account, and every answer can be revised when new evidence arrives.

In another institution, an appeal might never reach a competent receiver, or a witness might face retaliation for speaking. That is a broken correction route. Naming the break cannot force a remedy, but it can reveal why an apparent right to complain has not become an effective possibility.

The same connections can create something new. A neighbour shares a method, the child tries it, and the community makes tools and a meeting place available. Practice with useful feedback **may** form a capability, but retained performance on a different task and its continued support are the evidence to seek. The value of such a shared capability lies in what people can actually do and sustain, with room to refuse or change course.

Not every valuable future was waiting as an option in a menu. Someone grows into a new skill. Neighbours make a place where learning can happen. A scientist discovers something useful. A city builds safer streets. A damaged habitat is tended across generations. People find relationships, beauty and time for lives they did not know were possible. These gains have clocks, carriers, costs and maintenance just as injuries do. Their value does not make their burdens disappear.

Sometimes we must act quickly; sometimes we need another person's expertise; sometimes refusal is the appropriate choice. The question is which path remains possible for the affected people and what can honestly be done next.

The method must answer for its own selected view as well. This book chose the glass, the library and a few machine examples; people and situations outside those scenes may expose a missing boundary or a false connection. A reader can compare a claim with evidence, show where its example fails, or reject the claimed transfer. Those observations have a route back to the human editor, who can revise this research draft while retaining the earlier version. A correctly assembled PDF only shows that the pages were assembled; it does not show that readers understood the argument. This is the same distinction between record, witness and effective correction that the library taught us.

The purpose of this language is to help people see what changes, make needed correction reachable and create useful possibilities without losing sight of those who carry the work. The glass is still on the table; the child can now do something that was not possible at the beginning. The future remains open to what they will learn and choose next.

# Notes: Scope, Method, Limits, Sources and Authorship

## What the language does

Mechanical Ethics is an experimental, portable reasoning language for human and AI-assisted inquiry under limited knowledge. Its descriptive vocabulary helps locate changing conditions, boundaries, apertures, accounts, possible and usable routes, clocks, carriers, feedback and achieved effects. It can identify where a question, warning or repair must travel. A description alone cannot grant rights, produce moral priorities, prove motives or make a remedy happen.

The distinctions in the chapters are not a complete mathematical system. **A ≠ B** preserves two roles that should not be confused; it does not imply numerical inequality, or that A and B can never coincide in one case. A compositional arrow expresses a conditional route with missing premises still to be checked. The timing relation is about a specified threatened outcome and actual protective effect under supported clock bounds; it is neither a guarantee nor a moral permission. When a necessary premise is unknown, the result stays unknown.

## Values and responsible use

Judgements about harm, benefit, standing, priority, privacy and legitimate intervention require declared value sources and authority. The same mechanism can serve different purposes. A complete account should say who selected the affected scope, comparator and horizon, who can contest the choice, and which relevant people or entities may have been left outside it. Naming these inputs makes debate possible; it does not prove that the decision was just. Professional medicine, law, safety engineering and other specialist fields have stronger methods and duties that this language must not replace.

A record can support an investigation without being a lawful public carrier. Personal refusal, private information and witnesses' safety remain consequential. Case closure may coexist with unrepaired loss or an unchanged mechanism. A constructed opportunity may have hidden costs or collapse without maintenance. These cautions belong alongside particular choices in practice, not as automatic objections to every constructive possibility.
