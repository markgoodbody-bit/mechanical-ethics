# Mechanical Ethics — A World That Can Answer Back

**AI-assisted alternate human manuscript · research draft · 14 October 2026 · not written or accepted by Mark · not Mechanical Ethics v0.9 released.**

This is a complete alternative reading path, not a revision of the released v0.8 source. The book, its authorial record, illustrations and appendices remain separately preserved. This draft explores a different way of bringing a first-time reader into the same concerns: one unfolding line of understanding, not a sequence of exercises.

## Before the first page

If you are being harmed by a process now, do not take risks to make your experience fit the pages of this book. Your safety, your housing, your livelihood and the people who can actually help matter more than constructing a record. A record is not rescue. If gathering evidence, challenging an institution or speaking publicly would make your position more dangerous, safer human help takes priority. You do not owe this book a demonstration.

The people and events in the central thread are invented. They illustrate possibilities, not evidence that a particular policy or organisation works.

# 1. The Room

Before we ask what anyone ought to do, begin with a simpler fact.

You are somewhere.

Perhaps you are in a room, with a window or without one. There is a surface under you, an arrangement of objects nearby, a sound you have begun to ignore. If you look away from these words, you will notice some of those things and miss others. Even if you were able to name everything in sight, there would be more happening than you could see. Air would be moving. Something would be ageing. Outside the room another person might be making a decision that will change your afternoon.

You did not choose the whole arrangement you woke into. Some parts of it were built long before you arrived. Some are the results of your own past choices. A few can be changed now. Most cannot be changed by you alone.

This is not a bleak discovery. It is the ordinary starting position of a living being in a world that continues whether or not that being has a complete account of it.

You have a point of view. It is useful because it lets you find your shoes, recognise a voice, avoid a hot pan, remember a promise. It is limited because every point of view has an edge. Someone else can be looking at the same room and noticing what you missed. A child may see a hiding place under the table. A person with poor eyesight may hear that the tap has been dripping. A dog may follow a smell that means nothing to you. There are things each can sense and things each cannot.

We sometimes imagine that the cure for a limited view is simply more information. Information helps. But no amount of it will turn one position into every position. A map of the room is not the room. A camera is not an eye that sees the whole world. A report is not a life. A person who knows a great deal may still not know the one thing that matters to the person standing opposite them.

Now add time.

While you read, the room is changing. The tea cools. Daylight moves. A message waits. You might leave the tap running, or turn it off, or fail to hear it. Each action, including leaving things as they are, belongs to a sequence with consequences. Some consequences can be undone. Others cannot be undone in the same way. Spilled water can be wiped up. Water that has travelled into an electrical socket may have changed the problem.

We often make decisions without thinking of ourselves as deciding. We wait another day before phoning someone. We accept the default on a screen. We take the route we always take. The world does not mark those moments with dramatic music. It simply moves on.

Imagine the rain beginning against your window. At first it is ordinary weather. You might enjoy the sound. A gardener might be relieved. A child might look forward to splashing through the puddles. Farther along the street, water is already collecting at a crossing where the drain has been blocked for weeks.

The rain is the same rain. It does not arrive as the same event in every life.

That small difference is the first opening in the argument. Something happens. A being encounters only part of it. The being must continue to act within a changing world. Other beings are encountering the change from positions it cannot occupy. The consequences will not necessarily fall where the decisions are made.

There is no moral formula hiding in this opening. We have not said what a good person is, or what every valuable life should contain. We have only found the ground on which moral decisions actually occur: partial knowledge, movement, other lives and consequences.

The rest of the book will widen that picture. We will return to the same rain, the same street and the same question from positions farther apart. The details will grow more difficult. The beginning will remain recognisable.

# 2. Beyond the Window

The rain has been falling for an hour when Ada leaves her house.

She is seventy-three, though her age tells us less about the morning than the fact that she is using a walking frame. She needs to cross the main road for a medical appointment. The crossing has a raised kerb, a pedestrian signal and a refuge in the middle. Ordinarily it is manageable. Today the shallow gutter beside the kerb has become a wide sheet of brown water.

From the upstairs window of the house opposite, the water may look no worse than a puddle. From the height of a car it might barely register. Ada is looking at the uneven surface where the frame will have to stand. She cannot easily judge the depth. She has already learned, from an earlier fall, that recovering from a minor mistake can take much longer than making it.

A driver approaches. He has a reason to get through the green light. The parent in the seat beside him is late for work, and their child is watching the clock. Their impatience is not proof of malice. It is one more fragment of a world in which time and obligation are unevenly distributed.

A neighbour, Jo, sees Ada stop. Jo steps outside and asks whether help would be useful. Ada considers the offer. She might be grateful. She might also want to make the crossing independently. The answer belongs to her in a way that the existence of the puddle does not.

It would be easy to tell this as a story in which Jo is kind and the driver is selfish. That would be too neat. The driver may be carrying somebody to urgent care. Jo could make things worse by taking the walking frame without asking. Ada might need assistance today and decline it tomorrow. Even the useful act has to meet another person's reality rather than an image of that person.

To understand the scene, imagine turning in place.

From Jo's doorstep, the important feature is the person stopped at the kerb. From Ada's position, it is the water, the unsteady surface, the appointment and the possibility that accepting help may also mean surrendering control. From the driver's seat, there is a traffic signal, a line of vehicles and a road that appears open. From the council's maintenance office there may be a numbered drain, a scheduled inspection and no indication that a particular person is waiting beside it. From the rain's position—if it had one worth describing—there would be no distinction between a kerb, a frame and a file.

These are not equally empowered views. The maintenance office may send a crew. Jo can call a number. Ada can decide whether to enter the road, but she cannot repair the drain or move the appointment by wishing it so. The driver can stop, yet cannot settle the problem for everyone who will cross tomorrow.

When people meet, each brings a different view and a different ability to change things. An offer of assistance is useful only if it travels across that difference. So is a warning. So is a refusal.

Jo asks again, this time more specifically: would Ada like someone to stand beside her while she tests the kerb? Ada agrees. They cross slowly, without a fall. The appointment is still possible. The puddle is still there.

One small route has been preserved for one person. That matters. But a second person may reach the crossing after Jo goes inside. The ordinary act of kindness cannot substitute for a repair that should belong to someone with the authority and resources to make it.

This is where an individual story begins to lead outward. We are no longer looking only at what one person can see. We have begun to notice the arrangements that distribute sight, effort, permission and consequence.

Someone designed the crossing. Someone maintains the drain. Someone decides which reports are urgent. These people may never meet Ada. Their work nevertheless becomes part of the world she must navigate.

The scale has changed, but it has not erased the first discovery. The crossing is another kind of room: a bounded place with changing conditions, limited observers and actions that affect those who did not choose the arrangement. Yet the analogy has limits. A street belongs to public institutions, carries legal obligations and serves many people at once. It is not simply a household writ large. Shared structure helps us see; it must not be used to flatten the differences.

Ada reaches her appointment. Jo goes home wet. The driver disappears along the road. The water remains, and now there is a new possibility: somebody could tell the people responsible what has happened.

# 3. The Message

Jo photographs the crossing later that morning.

The photograph shows the water surrounding the kerb and part of the pedestrian light. It does not show how Ada's frame shook on the uneven ground. It cannot tell us whether the drain is blocked by leaves, whether the road surface slopes incorrectly or whether the next rainfall will be heavier. Still, it captures something worth communicating.

Jo finds the council's online reporting page. There is a box for a location, a category and a short description. The menu offers *flooding*, *blocked drain* and *damaged pavement*. Jo chooses blocked drain, although the problem that brought Jo to the page was a person unable to cross safely.

A reference number appears. The page says the report has been received.

That is a real event. It is not the same event as a repair.

We build forms because people need ways to send information without having to find the right official personally. A good form can help a report reach the correct team, preserve its date and prevent an issue from disappearing when a member of staff is absent. A form can also require the person sending the report to translate a complex experience into the categories the receiving organisation has already chosen.

Jo's photograph crosses the boundary successfully. Ada's morning does not. The time spent testing the water, the risk of a fall, the missed part of her appointment and the reason the crossing matters are more difficult to fit into the file.

Neither side is necessarily lying. The record may be accurate and incomplete. The person reading it may follow every rule and still receive too little of the situation to act well.

This distinction is common enough to be nearly invisible. A letter can say an appeal was available. A workplace system can show that training was completed. A school can record that a notice was sent. A hospital can confirm that a result entered the portal. These statements may all be true while leaving unanswered whether the intended person could use what was offered.

Imagine a door with a handle and a sign saying *Entrance*. If it opens only onto a staircase, a person using a wheelchair cannot pass through merely because an architect can point to it on a plan. The word *entrance* describes a claim about function. Its reality depends on who needs to use it and under what conditions.

Jo's report is another door of that kind. The council has provided a way to ask for help. Whether help can travel back through it is not settled by the presence of the submit button.

Ada does not know that Jo has filed the report. Jo does not know who has received it. The person assigned to the file may not know Ada exists. Nobody has yet done anything dishonest, yet important knowledge has been split between people who cannot easily answer one another.

Jo could add a message about the walking frame. Jo might worry about writing Ada's medical details into a system without her agreement. The fuller account may be more compelling, but another person's private life is not simply evidence waiting to be harvested. Jo asks Ada what she is comfortable including. Ada agrees to a description of the practical access problem, not her diagnosis or appointment history.

That choice illustrates something easily lost in the desire for complete records. Accuracy does not require unlimited exposure. A person can give enough information to make an issue answerable and keep other parts of life private. The institution should not need to own a person in order to repair a drain.

The revised report says the flooding prevents some pedestrians using the crossing safely. It asks whether there is a temporary safe route while repair is arranged. It carries a photograph, a location and the date. Those details are small, but they join two accounts that would otherwise remain apart.

The reply is automatic: the request has been logged. The council hopes to respond within its service period.

Jo closes the page. Outside, the rain eases. The water stays.

# 4. What Waiting Does

By the next morning the crossing is nearly dry.

The blocked drain is still blocked. A parent pushing a buggy goes around the puddle that remains. A cyclist rolls through it. Ada decides to use another route, one that adds twenty minutes and a steep hill. In the council's system the report remains open. It has not been rejected.

The distinction between an open file and an open route is now easier to see.

If the crew arrives next week, the report may be resolved within the published service period. If Ada misses a necessary appointment tomorrow, that service period will not protect the particular route she needed yesterday. The institution's clock and Ada's clock are both real, but only one is written into the usual performance measure.

Time does not operate as a neutral container around decisions. It changes what an action can still accomplish.

A friend can apologise for failing to arrive. That apology may repair trust if the failure was small, understood and not repeated. It will not make the missed final train run again. A refund can return money to an account while leaving untouched the interest paid to borrow in the meantime. A restored email address cannot guarantee that the job opportunity missed during a lockout will come back. A correct medical result can arrive after the useful treatment window has narrowed.

This is not an argument that later repair is worthless. It is an argument for being precise about what later repair can and cannot restore.

Imagine wet plaster drying into a distorted shape. While it is soft, a light touch can alter it. Later correction may require breaking it, rebuilding it and accepting that some material has been lost. Human situations are not literally plaster, but they also have paths that become progressively harder to change. Money is spent. Bodies are injured. Relationships acquire histories. A public accusation travels beyond its source. A family moves away from a school. The available future changes.

The practical question is not only *Will the decision eventually be corrected?* It is *What can still be protected by a correction arriving at that time?*

Ada has already changed her routine. To avoid the crossing she now leaves earlier and returns tired. A council officer, looking only at the next day's photograph, might conclude that the flooding was minor because no one was visibly stranded when the image was taken. The altered life can disappear from the measurement precisely because Ada has adapted to the hazard.

This is a peculiar danger of slow harm. People become skilled at enduring an avoidable problem, and their competence is then mistaken for evidence that the problem is harmless.

The burden falls unevenly. Somebody with a car may avoid the flooded path. Somebody with flexible work can arrive late. Somebody who cannot risk losing wages may walk through the water. The same delayed repair therefore changes different lives by different amounts. It can even appear successful from an office if the complaints grow fewer while those worst affected abandon the route.

The deadline the organisation sees might be Friday. The deadline the person lives with might be this afternoon. Neither should be invented to win an argument; they need to be discovered.

When Jo speaks to the maintenance office again, the question changes. Jo no longer asks only when the repair is scheduled. Jo asks whether someone has authority to place a temporary warning, to clear the immediate obstruction safely, or to arrange another accessible crossing while the underlying problem is investigated.

These are modest actions, not heroic solutions. They may also be unavailable for good reasons: emergency work has to be prioritised, staff cannot enter a dangerous site without protection, and a temporary crossing can create risks for traffic and other pedestrians. The answer has to come from people with actual competence and authority. A book about ethical structure does not give Jo permission to direct roadworks.

Yet the shape of the request matters. Jo is trying to make effective action arrive before the loss it is intended to prevent. The question has moved from a completed administrative step to a future still capable of change.

The council officer checks the location. An inspection can take place sooner, though the officer cannot guarantee a repair date. A temporary sign is considered. Ada may still need the longer route today. Something, however, has changed: the threatened ability to cross safely is now visible as a matter of time, not merely as an item waiting in a queue.

We can call this hardening when we need a short word for it. We can speak of a correction window when precision is useful. But the language comes after the experience. You already understand that a door left open after the last person has had to leave is not the same as a door that was reachable when it mattered.

# 5. Through Other People's Hands

The report passes through a series of people.

The first officer verifies the location. Another checks the work schedule. A contractor may need permission to close a lane. Someone responsible for traffic safety must consider what happens if workers or pedestrians are sent into the road. A finance team may approve expenditure. Each part has a purpose. Each person may be doing something necessary.

Still, the passage through these hands consumes time.

This is a strange property of large systems. Every step can make sense from the position of the person carrying it out, while the sequence as a whole makes less sense from the position of the person waiting for its result.

A maintenance team needs to avoid dangerous improvised repairs. The finance office needs to prevent misuse of public funds. The officer answering the telephone cannot promise what a contractor will do. Independent checks can protect people from an official's mistake. Removing every boundary in the name of speed could harm others.

But the cost of those safeguards is not automatically paid by the people who chose them. It may fall on Ada's legs, Jo's unpaid time, the driver redirected into another street or the child walking past a blocked kerb.

No one person needs to be cruel for that distribution to be unfair.

Suppose the council promises to answer every complaint in twenty working days. A manager can meet that target by sending a meaningful response near the deadline. Another can meet it with a standard acknowledgement that moves the file into a different queue. A dashboard may treat both as successes if the question being measured is merely whether an answer was sent.

The arrangement teaches its participants what counts. People with good intentions come to pay attention to the measurable event because it is the one their working lives are organised around. Slowly the record can begin to lead the work rather than describe it.

This does not make measurement useless. Without a record, a team may not know it has ignored the same street six times. The deeper difficulty is the mistaken promotion of a limited measure into a complete account of reality.

The people inside the institution are themselves bounded beings. The call handler may know nothing of the procurement contract. The engineer may know the physical defect and not the consequences of delay. The director may see a total cost and not an individual crossing. Each has a fragment. Their power differs, and the arrangement governing how fragments travel matters.

Think back to the room. You could see one portion of it; another person could see a different portion. Here the room has become a network of people, rules and machines. But we should not treat an organisation as if it has a single pair of eyes or one shared conscience. Organisations distribute authority deliberately. Some can hold budgets, make laws or compel action. They have duties a neighbour does not have and risks a neighbour cannot take on.

The useful resemblance lies in the pattern: a world changes, knowledge is partial, action passes through bounded channels, and consequences land beyond the point where they were chosen.

A responsible design therefore has to attend to the joints between positions. Does the person who sees a danger have a way to reach someone who can change it? Does the person who can change it hear what would make the intervention useful? Can a refusal travel backwards with a reason? Is there a route for a mistaken classification to be corrected before the next automated step?

The answer is rarely to remove all structure. It is to make important crossings between parts of the structure more reliable.

During the inspection, an engineer notices that the drain is blocked not only by leaves but by a distorted cover. A quick clearing would help until the next heavy storm. A lasting repair needs a different team. This is new information, gained through the specialist knowledge that the complaint form did not contain. It is precisely the kind of expertise the wider system ought to make useful.

The engineer records both facts: what can be done now, and what the temporary fix does not settle. The first makes the crossing safer. The second keeps the unresolved mechanism from vanishing beneath a green completion marker.

Good coordination does not require everyone to know everything. It requires the right distinctions to survive the journey between them.

# 6. A Trace That Survives

The temporary work is completed.

Water begins to run into the drain again. Ada uses the crossing that afternoon. The engineer's note says the cover still needs replacement. Jo's original photograph is stored with the complaint. The public website now shows a completed action.

What happens to these records matters.

A photograph is often treated as proof because it appears to show an event directly. In fact it shows part of an event from a particular position at a particular moment. It can be powerful evidence of the water's extent. It cannot tell us everything that happened to Ada, or whether the engineer's later repair will hold through another storm.

A timestamp can say when a file was received by a system. It does not, by itself, guarantee that the date was set by an independent and trustworthy clock. A checksum can show that a retained file matches another set of bytes. If the only earlier copy and the checksum were both controlled by a party able to alter them, the comparison may say little about what existed before the alteration.

Records need not be perfect to be useful. But their limitations should not be hidden by the impressive appearance of technical certainty.

Jo keeps a copy of the original report. The council keeps its own log. Ada has her memory of the crossing and the changed route she took the following day. The engineer has notes about the physical defect. These accounts overlap. They do not become one account merely because a case number joins them.

If a dispute arises, the ability to compare independent records can matter. If a provider can change both the claim and its history, an error may become difficult to contest. If a witness can publish every private detail about Ada, the price of contesting the error may become too high. Independence, availability, consent and custody are not decorative properties of evidence. They determine whether evidence can be used responsibly.

Suppose the council later reports that the problem was fixed permanently. Jo could point to the engineer's note showing the remaining defect. The note would not establish every fact about the repair, but it could keep a crucial question open. If the record is lost, the same question may have to be raised again after somebody else encounters the water.

This is one reason why correction requires more than a way to send a complaint. The institution must retain enough of the disagreement to make the next decision better.

And the next decision belongs to the future, not to the archive.

We sometimes speak as though a well-kept record makes the original harm acceptable. It does not. The person who crossed unsafely cannot be compensated by perfect metadata alone. The record matters because it may help prevent repetition, support a fair remedy, expose a false claim or let affected people answer a more powerful account.

Imagine a person writing down an argument after it has ended. If the note helps the people involved understand what went wrong and behave differently next time, it has served a purpose. If the note is used to preserve one side's authority forever, it may become part of the harm. The technology of memory is less important than what memory allows people to do.

This book is itself a record, and it has the same limitations. It can show patterns, preserve a distinction and invite criticism. It cannot turn an invented street into evidence of a real council's conduct. It cannot settle the rights of people whose circumstances are not here. It cannot certify that the ideas on its pages are ethically correct because the language is systematic.

The claim is smaller and more useful: honest traces can keep reality available for later correction if the people affected can meaningfully challenge what those traces are taken to mean.

One person's notebook can serve that purpose within a relationship. An independent clinical record can matter in medicine. A protected engineering report can matter in a large technical organisation. The means and obligations differ. The reason for preserving the distinction recurs.

Outside, the street has dried. In the council's files, the first problem has a completion date. In the engineer's notes, another decision is waiting. In Ada's life, the missed time will never become an unspent morning again.

# 7. The Next Person

Several weeks later, the rain returns.

This time the blocked cover catches debris sooner than it did before. Jo is away. Ada takes another street. A teenager on a bicycle rides into the pooled water and has to swerve. The incident is brief. There is no photograph, and nobody submits a new report.

The council's record of the earlier job is not false. Work was done. The crossing improved. The problem returned because an identified defect remained.

There are two questions now. Was the first person helped? Did the arrangement learn enough to help the next?

We often stop at the first question because it is easier to answer. A refund was paid. A repair was completed. An apology was issued. An account was restored. The person who brought the complaint may be satisfied, exhausted or determined never to deal with the institution again. Each outcome deserves attention on its own terms.

But a process can repair an individual file while leaving intact the mechanism that created the trouble. The next person enters the same route with no knowledge of the previous warning. For that person, the institution has not learned anything that matters.

Jo's report was never supposed to create a permanent duty for Jo to watch the street. The engineer knew the cover needed replacement. If that knowledge cannot influence maintenance planning, the system has treated its own discovery as disposable.

The distance between recording a lesson and changing future action is where organisations often fail.

After a serious error, the language of reform comes readily. We will review our procedures. We will improve training. We will appoint an owner. We will update the dashboard. Any of these may be necessary. None tells us, on its own, whether the next person will encounter a different world.

To answer that, the repair must travel into the design of the work: an updated schedule, a funded replacement, an accessible temporary route, clearer ownership of the unresolved defect, or a way for another report to reopen the decision without repeating every earlier argument.

Sometimes the new arrangement should be tested before a permanent change. A drain repaired incorrectly can flood a different street. A safety measure for one group may create a new barrier for another. Good intentions do not exempt a correction from having consequences.

This is why the continuing record should include what remains uncertain. The engineer may be confident that replacing the cover will solve the immediate obstruction, less certain about the road's drainage during exceptional rainfall, and unable to predict the effect of a wider redevelopment. Those are different claims. Compressing them into *issue resolved* would make the institution sound more certain than its evidence permits.

A person might ask whether this attention to the next case has anything to do with ethics. Surely it is just competent maintenance.

There is no reason to separate those things sharply. Engineering, medicine, public administration and care already contain hard-won methods for avoiding recurrent harm. Ethical language should not replace that expertise. It can help carry an important question across domains: when an institution learns that one of its arrangements is harming someone, what must change before the next person arrives?

The answer is not always a new rule. Sometimes it is better staffing, a different threshold, an independent reviewer, a new route for urgent work or the removal of a rule that imposes more burden than it prevents. In every case the change has to be answerable to those who will live with it.

A public service cannot promise a risk-free world. It can be expected not to treat known and correctable defects as someone else's private problem merely because the last complaint has been closed.

The crossing is now part of a larger story. It belongs to a pattern of roads, budgets, maintenance contracts, safety decisions, weather and mobility. A return visit reveals whether yesterday's lesson has become a property of tomorrow's environment.

You could make the same observation in a family without declaring the family identical to a council. Someone says, *I am sorry I interrupted you.* That matters. If the same interruption continues at every meal, the apology has not yet changed the environment in which the other person tries to speak. Repair of a moment and repair of a repeated arrangement are different achievements.

One person, the next person, and the conditions both inherit. The pattern is beginning to repeat at more than one scale.

# 8. The Weight of Power

It is tempting to describe everybody in this story as merely doing their best with incomplete information. That would conceal something important.

The people involved do not have equal power.

Ada cannot allocate a repair budget. Jo cannot compel an inspection. The first officer may be able to send a message but not authorise roadworks. The engineer can identify a hazard but might be unable to purchase the replacement cover. The director can change priorities within limits imposed by law, funding and other duties.

Power is not always exercised by someone issuing an order. It can live in the ability to set the menu, choose the deadline, retain the record, define what counts as a completed repair or decide how many times a person must repeat the same story.

A person with little power may spend a remarkable amount of effort making a simple problem visible. A powerful institution may only need to mark a field to declare that its part is finished. When this happens, difficulty itself becomes a cost pushed outward from the decision-maker to the person least able to carry it.

This does not justify assuming that every complicated process is designed to exploit someone. Some barriers protect privacy, public safety or equal treatment. The distinction emerges when we ask who can challenge the arrangement and whether the challenge can actually change it.

Suppose a complaint requires a photograph, an online account, a reference number and a response within five working days. Each requirement might have a defensible reason. Together they could exclude the person whose phone is broken, who cannot read the notice, who has no stable address, or who is frightened of a data trail. The opportunity to complain might exist formally and be unusable in practice.

A powerful party is not necessarily wrong because it is powerful. It becomes less answerable when the people affected cannot get a consequential account across the boundary of its power.

The street has another lesson about authority. Jo can support Ada, but Jo should not take control of Ada's choices. Consent to stand beside someone is not consent to publish their medical information or to become their permanent representative. Support can gradually turn into possession if the helper begins to believe that being useful grants a right to decide.

Institutions can make the same mistake at greater scale. A system built to protect people can collect more information than protection requires. It can turn exceptional access into a permanent store. It can quietly make refusal the path that loses a service. It can announce success in the language of the people who run it while making disagreement difficult for everyone else.

The cure is not the fantasy of a world without influence. Human beings depend on one another. Decisions require delegated power. Some emergencies cannot wait for every participant to agree. The more useful aim is to make power carry the cost of hearing correction, to require reasons appropriate to the decision, and to preserve legitimate ways of refusing, appealing, leaving or redesigning the relationship.

These routes will not always work. An unjust or indifferent ruler can ignore a truthful witness. A monopolist can keep customers with no practical alternative. A frightened person may be unable to contest an official account safely. Telling such people to document harder would only place more weight on them.

Where power refuses correction, the record of that refusal is still important, but it is not enough. Collective organisation, legal protection, independent institutions, democratic authority, professional duties and material support may be needed. There is no universal shortcut.

The question that began at the window has acquired a harder edge. We know that observers see only part of a changing world. Now we know that some observers can make their partial view compulsory for others.

That is where an honest description of a system becomes a moral concern. Not because the system can eliminate uncertainty, but because it has power to act despite uncertainty and must answer for what its actions do.

# 9. More Speed Than Attention

Imagine the council introduces a new service to handle maintenance reports.

An artificial system reads photographs and descriptions, groups similar requests, estimates urgency and routes them to staff. It might find repeated flooding reports faster than the old queue. It could compare the engineer's earlier note with a new photograph and bring the remaining defect to a supervisor's attention. Used well, it might shorten the time between a person's warning and effective work.

That is a real possibility worth pursuing.

Now imagine that the system learns to treat clear images of deep water as urgent, and blurry or incomplete images as low priority. A hurried person using an old phone produces a poor image. A frightened person describes their problem badly. A street at greatest risk to the least mobile residents is photographed only after the water begins to drain. The model may become fast at doing the wrong thing to precisely the people whose cases are hardest to express in the form it expects.

No machine needs to want harm for that pattern to arise. A choice about what counts as evidence, joined to the speed of automation, can turn a small mismatch into repeated exclusion.

The new system is another bounded participant in the arrangement, though not a human participant. It receives selected inputs, operates within a design, and produces effects. Its access to reality is shaped by sensors, training, interfaces, tools and permissions. It may be excellent at detecting patterns across thousands of reports and poor at understanding what one person could not write.

The mistake would be to imagine either that the machine knows everything because it can process so much, or that it can contribute nothing because it does not occupy Ada's position.

The important question is what the arrangement lets people do with its output.

Can the clerk see why a request was routed to low priority? Can the engineer correct the classification? Does the system preserve the original warning or replace it with the model's summary? Can a person appeal before an automatic closure causes a practical loss? Does a human who can actually change the outcome remain reachable? Is there enough time for that person to intervene?

Speed changes the relationship between mistake and correction. A human clerk may make ten poor classifications during a shift. An automated system may make ten thousand before anyone notices a shared error. Automation can also detect and correct thousands of similar defects that a small team would never find by hand. Both possibilities come from scale, and neither can be wished away with a slogan about technology.

A design that includes an effective stop is not necessarily timid. It may be what allows greater capability to be used without concentrating unanswerable harm. The stop has to be real, however. A button that pauses one display while the consequential decision continues elsewhere is a symbol, not a brake.

People often speak about a human being *in the loop*. That phrase can hide the actual arrangement. A person who is asked to approve five thousand classifications per hour has nominal authority and almost no practical capacity to exercise it. A person who can inspect the evidence, challenge patterns and halt a class of decisions may have far more meaningful oversight while touching fewer individual files.

What matters is not the ceremonial presence of a human. It is whether some accountable actor can understand enough, act soon enough and change what the system does.

As artificial systems become more capable, new questions appear about dependence and relationship. A helper may teach someone how to repair a bicycle, translate an instruction or plan a route. It may also make users lose the confidence or means to work without it. A provider can change the model or withdraw the service. A volunteer who once carried local knowledge can become a person who simply repeats what the system says.

Those outcomes do not make machine assistance inherently wrong. They remind us that useful capability includes the ability to question, replace or leave the helper.

Questions about whether some present or future artificial entities might have experiences or standing are unresolved and should not be decided by flattery or dismissal. Whatever those later answers prove to be, power over other entities is already consequential. We can build arrangements that preserve correction and honest uncertainty now, without pretending that every philosophical dispute has been settled.

Back at the council, the new routing system notices that the earlier flooding report and the engineer's unresolved replacement note describe the same location. This is the useful thing it was meant to do. A maintenance planner checks the evidence, confirms the outstanding work and schedules the correct repair.

The machine has neither rescued the world nor betrayed it. It has helped connect a piece of knowledge to a person with authority in time for an ordinary improvement.

The result is unglamorous. Ada will notice it most on the next wet morning.

# 10. A Place That Wasn't There

The repaired drain makes a street usable again. That is worth having.

Yet a life made only of avoided injury would be a thin life. People want more than the absence of danger. They want to learn, make things, love, rest, explore, belong, disagree, enjoy privacy and sometimes invent possibilities that did not exist when they began.

Return to Jo.

A few neighbours have been meeting in an unused room at the community hall. At first the room was a place to share concerns about the street. A maintenance worker brought a drawing of the drainage plan, and somebody else brought a photograph from the last storm. They understood the immediate problem better. The conversation might have ended there.

Instead a teenager asked whether the hall could open on Saturdays for repairs. Several people had bicycles with minor problems they could not afford to take to a shop. A retired mechanic offered to show volunteers how to check brakes. Another neighbour could teach basic computer repairs. Somebody else wanted a quiet table where children could work while parents were occupied.

This is no longer simply a way to prevent a flooded crossing. It is an attempt to make a place that was not there before.

The possibility feels attractive. It also has costs that a cheerful poster will not show.

The room needs heat, safe equipment, accessible toilets, insurance and someone able to decide what happens when a repair goes wrong. The mechanic cannot be expected to donate every Saturday indefinitely. A volunteer who cares for a family member cannot promise the same hours each week. Free access requires actual money from somewhere. Some people will not want their names or photographs published to demonstrate the project's success.

If the hall boasts that twelve people attended its first session, the number will not tell us whether anybody learned, whether someone had to leave because of the noise, or whether the bus route made attendance impossible for the people the project most wanted to include.

Those observations need not kill the idea. They turn imagination into design.

A small first session may be worthwhile even if it cannot become a national programme. The organiser might reserve a quiet space, agree that people can decline photographs, pay skilled instructors when possible and keep a route for participants to suggest changes. The aim would not be a perfect plan. It would be a real opportunity that can be used, sustained and altered without quietly consuming the people who maintain it.

In the flooded-street story we asked whether a route existed in practice, not merely on paper. The same question returns here in a happier form. Does the promised opportunity become something a person can actually reach? Can it endure without unreasonable hidden costs? Can those affected reshape it? Is the work itself becoming a place of learning and connection, rather than another institution whose success is declared by its owners?

The familiar pattern has not confined us to danger. It has made positive ambition more exact.

There is a difference between saving a path that was open, finding a path nobody had noticed and building a path that did not previously exist. They all matter. Each may create new dependencies and close other possibilities. A decision to spend the hall's money on repair tools means not spending it on another worthwhile activity. Such choices belong to the people and institutions with legitimate authority, informed by those who will carry the consequences.

Consider the imaginative appeal of abundant futures. Science fiction offers societies with energy, leisure, knowledge and cooperation beyond our present arrangements. These stories can remind us that current scarcity and drudgery are not necessarily permanent features of human existence. They can invite curiosity about what people might choose to create if more of their time were genuinely their own.

But a horizon is not an instruction. No imagined civilisation grants permission to impose a single idea of happiness on the people living here. The useful distance between fiction and today is filled by ordinary work: food and housing, education, trustworthy institutions, energy, maintenance, conservation, culture, relationships and the material conditions that make refusal and exploration possible.

A future worth approaching should make room for somebody who loves engineering and somebody who wants to sit quietly by a lake. It should not require them to justify their lives as inputs to one approved objective.

The hall does not become a utopia. Perhaps the first Saturday is noisy, two donated computers fail and fewer people arrive than expected. Perhaps a child repairs a bicycle for the first time, and a neighbour who had never spoken to the mechanic stays for tea. Some value has been created. Some burdens are now clearer. The next session can change.

What began as an effort to make a hazard visible has made a different question available: what else could the community make possible if knowledge, time and resources could travel more fairly between people?

Mechanical Ethics would be an impoverished idea if it could only tell us how to stop. Seeing harm sooner matters because there are worthwhile futures to protect. Seeing our arrangements clearly also matters because those arrangements can be improved, not merely defended.

# 11. The Fire Beyond the Street

The crossing belongs to a neighbourhood. The neighbourhood belongs to a city. The city depends on water, energy, food, transport, law, labour, climate and decisions made far away.

It is possible to follow these connections outward until the whole world seems to be one vast machine. That picture has its dangers.

A city is not a body, and a population is not a single person with one desire. Some things that are ethical between friends would be coercive as public policy. The privacy owed to an individual cannot be replaced by the claim that all information benefits society. A nation can make agreements that bind people who never consented individually, but this creates obligations of representation and contestability that a friendship does not require. Similarities in structure do not cancel differences in legitimacy, power and experience.

Still, there is a recognisable recurrence.

Everywhere we look, a changing world meets bounded observers. Information passes between them imperfectly. Some actors can choose on behalf of others. Time changes the possibilities that remain. Records may help correct errors or preserve them. Repeated behaviour alters the environment the next being will inherit.

In a family, these relations may be expressed in conversation and trust. In a hospital, they may depend on professional duties, timely tests and independent clinical judgement. In an engineering organisation, they may involve warnings, design reviews, accountable stop authority and the physical behaviour of a system. In government, they also require public law, rights, democratic disagreement and attention to unequal resources. In an AI-supported service, they involve model limits, data, providers, operators, users and people affected without ever opening the interface.

It is the recurrence of relationships, not identity of objects, that makes a portable language potentially useful.

This language must remain humble about its reach. Domain specialists know things no general pattern can replace. A drainage engineer can identify a physical defect that an ethical theory cannot see. A lawyer can tell us which protections exist and who has authority. A nurse may detect a risk that a general account of correction misses. A person directly affected knows costs that none of the specialists carry in the same way.

The useful contribution is to help those forms of knowledge find one another without treating any one as complete.

Sometimes this is a matter of noticing the absent witness. If a policy review contains only the managers who administer a service, whose account cannot reach it? Sometimes it is noticing the wrong clock. If a regulatory appeal takes a year, what valuable path may close while the institution is still deciding? Sometimes it is noticing a hidden carrier. If a public record can be changed by the party it describes, how can another person later contest the account? Sometimes it is noticing the positive future excluded from a debate framed entirely as avoiding the last failure.

None of these questions answers itself. They help us locate where the real work belongs.

Power can produce improvements at enormous scale. Safe drinking water, reliable electricity, public transport, mass education and medicine require coordination far beyond what a neighbour can provide alone. The ability to build such things is part of the reason to care about answerability. A well-used institution can carry gifts into lives its builders will never meet.

Power can also harden mistakes over enormous distances. A bad rule can be copied into thousands of decisions. A model can compress a prejudice into a routine classification. An unchallengeable historical record can become the starting point for later punishment. The fact that a system was intended to do good does not exempt it from watching what actually happens.

At this scale the word *harm* becomes both important and dangerous. It is important because costs disappear when we count only the institution's own successful outputs. It is dangerous when someone treats a single harm score as authority to decide every value conflict for everyone. Some losses are necessary for worthwhile commitments; resources are finite; public interests genuinely compete. People differ in what they value. A framework can expose consequential trade-offs without acquiring the right to settle every one of them.

We therefore return to the other fire: another being, somewhere beyond our view, with a situation we cannot fully occupy. We might communicate. We might cooperate. We might disagree. We might lack a safe way to reach them. Their refusal, when legitimate, is not a defect to be optimised away.

An ethical language should help us travel farther without pretending that travel makes every place ours.

# 12. The Window Again

On the next wet morning, Ada approaches the crossing.

The surface is clear. The cover has been replaced. Water runs where it should. A new notice near the crossing explains how to report a fault, including a telephone route for somebody who cannot use the website. The maintenance team has kept a note of what failed and what was changed. The improvements may hold through the next storm. They might not. The world will have to tell us.

Ada crosses.

Perhaps nobody else pays attention. A child waits for the pedestrian light. A bus passes. Someone in a nearby office opens a spreadsheet. A community hall prepares for its second Saturday session, with more work to do than the first poster promised.

Return to the room where we began. There was a person inside it, aware of some things, ignorant of others, while the world continued to change.

The person can now see more without possessing the whole picture.

A puddle is no longer only water at a kerb. It is also a meeting of bodies, surfaces, weather, design, obligation and time. A report is no longer simply a form that was completed. It is an attempt to make one limited account reach another position with power to act. A successful repair is not merely a green mark in a file. It is an altered condition, a consequence in someone's life, and perhaps a change in what the next person will inherit.

A record matters, but it is not the event. A witness matters, but it is not every witness. A deadline matters because life continues while decisions are delayed. A powerful actor may have less knowledge of the relevant harm than the person who has almost no power to stop it. A helpful machine may widen what people can do, provided the means to understand and correct it are not lost. A community may build something genuinely new if it can see the people and resources on which that possibility depends.

These are not twelve instructions to memorise. They are aspects of the same changing situation, now seen through widening windows.

We cannot prevent all harm, know every future or settle every disagreement. A framework that promised those things would become another source of false confidence. We can often do something more modest and more consequential. We can notice when a decision is closing possibilities that matter. We can find who is carrying the cost. We can ask whether an answer can reach the right place before it is too late. We can keep the record honest enough to be corrected and the people affected present enough to answer back.

And we can build.

We can design doors that the people who need them can use. We can build institutions that learn from the next person rather than merely apologising to the last. We can share skills, make tools, restore habitats, protect private life, care for one another and create places that did not exist before. We can test these efforts without demanding that their beneficiaries become unpaid proof of success.

A future is not better simply because it is more technologically impressive, more orderly or easier to score. It is better, in the sense this book has been reaching for, when worthwhile possibilities become more real without quietly making the people who live among them less able to understand, refuse, repair and shape what comes next.

The first room is still a room. Its window is still limited. The world beyond it has not become simple.

But the person looking out has learned to ask a better question: not only *What am I seeing?*, but *What is changing beyond my view, who can answer, what might harden, and what could we make possible together?*

That is a beginning, not a final judgement. It is enough to return to the world and continue.
