## An ordinary navigator

Imagine you are going to meet a friend on the other side of town.

You know where you are and roughly where you want to go. You have travelled there before. You have enough time to take a familiar route, but not so much that the journey can take forever. You remember which streets are quiet, where the crossings are, and where there is a shop if it begins to rain.

You do not know everything along the way.

A footbridge may be closed. The next bus may be late. A gate that stood open last week may be locked today. Your phone can show a route, but the picture was made from information gathered at other times, by other people, for purposes that may not match yours.

Nothing dramatic has to happen for the map and the world to become different.

Suppose you reach the bridge and find a barrier. You could wait, turn back, use the longer street, or ask somebody coming from the other direction. None of those choices guarantees that you will arrive on time. Each uses something you have — time, attention, money, knowledge, patience — and changes what will still be possible a little later.

Waiting is not an escape from the decision. While you wait, the buses move, the rain changes, your friend makes plans, and other people continue their journeys. Doing nothing does not stop the world. Equally, turning back is not necessarily a failure. Perhaps you have learned enough to know that crossing today would carry too much risk or cost. Perhaps your friend would rather meet tomorrow.

Now somebody approaches from the other side of the barrier.

They say that a second path is open. You can ask how they know. Did they walk it a moment ago, or see it last week? You may take their advice, check part of it yourself, or decline. They may be mistaken. They may have a different destination. If they offer to walk with you, both of you may find a route that neither would have managed alone. You might help them with a heavy bag while they show you a turning.

That cooperation could help. It is not owed. Their knowledge does not make them the owner of your journey, and your need for help does not give you ownership of their time.

You might reach your friend by a new route. You might arrive late. You might spend the afternoon somewhere neither of you expected and be pleased with it. The measure of success is not simply the number of streets still available. A detour can be worthwhile; a commitment can close alternatives on purpose; another person's route may be as important to them as yours is to you.

This small journey contains most of the questions that matter here. You begin somewhere in an already-changing world. You can see part of it and remember part of it. You have a direction, but the map is incomplete. Other beings have their own directions, abilities and limits. Choices take time and effort. Some routes can be checked or corrected; others close before the correction arrives.

Sometimes the most useful act is to ask. Sometimes it is to give accurate information without making the other person follow it. Sometimes it is to keep a crossing open long enough for someone else to reach it. Sometimes it is to leave.

The world does not present one perfect route, and Mechanical Ethics cannot choose a person's destination for them. It asks whether we can see enough of this moving, shared situation to navigate more responsibly — and correct ourselves when we learn that our picture was wrong.

## Where the small journey stops being enough

It would be a mistake to think that every person travels with the same freedom.

One person has a phone, spare money, strong legs and the ability to take a day off. Another does not. One can safely ask a stranger for help. Another may be exposed by the request. Someone may control the only gate or decide whether the person asking will keep their home or work.

A route can exist on a map without being a route that a particular being can use.

That is where the ordinary navigation pattern becomes an ethical question. It is no longer enough to say that there were several possible roads. We must ask who could find them, afford them, pass through them, refuse them, and change them when they failed. We must also ask who was expected to carry the cost of a bad map.

Consider an existing scene from the released Mechanical Ethics reader. It is a **composite**, not a report about a named family.

Leah wakes because her daughter Mia is coughing again. There is damp behind the bed. Leah takes a photograph and opens her landlord's reporting page. The page accepts a reference number, a category and an image. It has no field for the fear of losing the tenancy, the work shift approaching in six hours, or the fact that the child is still in the room.

The system has a map of the event. Leah has another.

Both may contain true information. They do not contain the same information, and they do not have the same power. Leah can send a request; the landlord may be able to authorise repair, delay it, or set other consequential terms. The wall continues to change while the request sits in a queue.

Leah is not merely a traveller who failed to choose the best road. She is living inside a route somebody else largely designed, at a cost she did not choose, with a clock that will not pause merely because the form says *pending*.

Mechanical Ethics asks whether the route was actually usable by the person who needed it. Could she make the system understand the part of the situation that its categories missed? Could somebody with authority protect Mia's room while the cause was investigated? Could Leah challenge a mistaken record without losing the tenancy or spending resources she did not have?

And if the wall is eventually repaired, what happened during the weeks before the repair arrived?

The repair is real. So may be the lost sleep, money, trust or health. A corrected record does not send time backwards. But a changed process might spare the next family the same journey.

This is why the framework begins with navigation, then keeps going. The physical world, the records, the people and the institutions are connected. Their knowledge is unequal. Their power is unequal. The routes available to them can change the possibilities available to others.

Neither a map nor a formula can decide what Mia's life is worth, what sacrifices Leah owes, or whether a particular actor has the right to intervene. Those questions need human judgement, evidence and legitimate authority. The structure can help expose where the question has been hidden, but it cannot answer it on another being's behalf.

The direction of this book is therefore practical: **help the beings carrying the consequences remain present in the decisions that shape their futures.** Keep the relevant routes usable where possible. Keep evidence and correction able to reach the decision while they can still matter. Do not make somebody else carry all the work of a complexity you control. And when a path truly closes, do not rename the loss as though a good account had repaired it.

That is a beginning, not a completed moral theory.

## What this working opening must NOT imply

- **No novelty claim:** released ME v0.8.0 already starts with a bounded entity; this draft changes emphasis/scene, not the fact of an entity-first opening.
- **No fixed destination:** values, goals, acceptable futures and affected scopes must be authored and contestable; no single scalar “least friction” defines the right route.
- **No compulsory optimism:** an open alternative does not repair a closed relationship or impose an obligation to keep hoping.
- **No “always cooperate”:** another entity can help, constrain, exploit or prefer to be left alone. Consent/exit/burden must remain visible.
- **No victim-as-expert requirement:** Leah should not have to become a specialist in her landlord's system; practical action and protective assistance matter more than completing a diagnostic worksheet.
- **No pause-as-universal-default:** protective holds need evidence and real authority; delaying an already-authorised protective move can itself be harmful.
- **No identity or experience claim for artificial systems:** describing a bounded AI aperture supplies neither consciousness nor entitlement.
- **No replacement of the original Leah passage:** the existing 1:17 a.m. composite, its image/figure and its later interlude remain preserved and authoritative as v0.8 source.
- **No field-validation claim:** this wording has not been read by an independently recruited human or compared fairly with the released opening.

## Editorial and cold-reader test protocol (unrun)

**Comparator is essential.** Present this draft and the original released v0.8.0 opening in balanced order with a matched reading-time budget. The question is not “did the reader like it?” but “did this version let a cold reader recover one material distinction with lower burden than the original?” Do not retroactively claim novelty if both achieve the same result.

Ask without coaching or soliciting personal harm disclosure:

1. What has changed in the world before and while the traveller decides? If the reader infers the world freezes on “wait,” the opening failed.
2. Does the reader know who chose the destination, who could refuse help, and why an offered shortcut does not confer authority?
3. In Leah's case, can the reader identify at least one true thing in the file and one in her lived situation, without dismissing either as unreal?
4. Can the reader distinguish formal route, usable route, timely protection, repaired file and restored future? If all become “the complaint process,” the bridge failed.
5. Does the text make the reader feel compelled to solve all paths before taking an affordable protective step? If yes, reduce its analytical burden.
6. Does the reader retain Part VI's positive possibility — friends learning together, worthwhile commitments, kindness constrained by agency — rather than treating Mechanical Ethics only as a theory of institutional failure?
7. Which sentence was confusing, patronising, overly abstract or too long? What would they remove without losing a distinction?
8. Could the reader name a specialist owner (doctor, lawyer, engineer, community advocate) who should lead a particular real-world decision instead of the framework?

**Adverse outcomes count:** identical comprehension to v0.8 with more reading burden means no earned usability gain; better fluency with worse authority/value distinctions is a failure; praise without a demonstrated difference is not evidence.
