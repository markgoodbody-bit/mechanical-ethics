# A way through a changing world

*Mechanical Ethics — v0.9 complete human-reader research candidate, Build aperture, 13 October 2026*

**Working manuscript — not released, not validated, not a replacement for Mechanical Ethics v0.8.0 or TRACE v0.4.0.** The existing released book remains intact. This is a proposed beginning-to-end route through its subject, with new transitions and emphasis, not a declaration that its earlier structure was wrong. Human authorship, acceptance, eventual editing and release remain with Mark.

## Before you begin

If something is hurting you now, do not put yourself or anybody else at greater risk to satisfy the demands of a theory. You do not need to gather photographs, confront an institution, disclose private facts or follow the exercises in this text. Records are not rescue. Urgent medical, legal, safeguarding or other suitable human support can matter more than an elegant diagnosis. Nor should a person already carrying harm be required to become an expert in the system responsible for it.

This book offers a way of looking. It does not certify decisions as ethical, assign moral standing to a person or machine, grant permission to intervene, or claim that all meaningful lives share one destination. You may disagree with it, use a small part of it, or leave it aside.

Its central question is simple to ask and difficult to settle:

**How can we make a better future?**

Not only how we prevent something bad from happening. Not only how we correct yesterday's mistake. How can bounded people and other entities find, make, share, protect and sometimes transform possibilities worth having, without turning somebody else's future into our property?

## 1. Begin somewhere

Suppose you are travelling to meet a friend. You know where you are and have some idea of where you are going. You remember a road that worked before. You have time, but not unlimited time. Perhaps you have a phone. Perhaps its battery is low. Perhaps the bus route you expected is still on the map but no longer runs at this hour.

Before you move, the world is moving. The weather changes, people come and go, your friend receives a call, the traffic builds, somebody closes a gate. You can move, wait, ask, refuse a lift or suggest meeting elsewhere. Every one of those choices uses time in a world that does not suspend itself for your decision. Even doing nothing is something that takes place while other things happen.

There is a useful difference between a destination and a journey. Pointing towards a place does not establish that a path exists. A path marked on a map is not necessarily open. An open path may be too steep, costly, dangerous or obscure for the particular traveller. Knowing there is a path somewhere is not the same as knowing which way to turn in the few seconds before a choice must be made.

Nobody begins with every relevant fact. We act from partial views, memories and reports. Often that is enough. Sometimes the missing fact is the entire difference between arriving and becoming stranded.

An ordinary navigator does not solve the whole city before turning a corner. They look at what matters now, make a supported move, watch what changes and revise. The point is not endless vigilance. It is to avoid mistaking the picture carried in one's head for the streets themselves.

The same pattern can help when the journey concerns a school, workshop, service, relationship, institution or technical system. But the analogy has a limit: people are not merely vehicles in each other's maps, and their destinations cannot always be reduced to one agreed answer.

## 2. The other journeys

At the crossing another person offers directions. She saw a bus ten minutes ago, she says. Does that mean it is coming now? She may be reporting exactly what she saw, and still not know the answer. Another passer-by reports that the bridge is shut. He may have walked there moments ago or be repeating an old notice. Both pieces of information can be helpful without having the same reliability.

You might compare them, ask one more question, or take the familiar road. You also have to consider what asking costs. You could be holding up somebody on their way to work. You might be safe talking to strangers; another traveller may not be. When a person depends on somebody who controls their housing, care or job, even a friendly offer can feel like an instruction.

Look from the other side for a moment. The stranger has a direction, incomplete information and a future too. Their knowledge might open your route, but your need does not give you a claim on their time, body, history or private thoughts. They may help freely. They may decline without having to defend their whole life.

It is tempting to turn a helpful meeting into the rule that cooperation always makes things better. That is false. A companion can warn you of danger, distract you at a crossing, demand a detour, or offer a shortcut that exists only for them. A group can pool skills and still put the largest risk on its least powerful member. The fact of being connected establishes a relationship, not its goodness.

Yet connection sometimes makes a genuinely new route possible. One person has a key, another a tool, a third the knowledge to use it. The resulting possibility is not simply the sum of three private maps. Whether it should be pursued still depends on consent, risk, fair burden and the purposes of the people involved.

We shall keep returning to that difference: **another entity is not only information in our model of the world. It has a position of its own.**

## 3. The road that did not exist yesterday

A map can make us think that the task of a navigator is merely to select a road from a list.

Consider learning to repair a bicycle. On Monday there may be no usable path from a broken wheel to a working bicycle through your own hands. By Saturday you may have acquired a tool, watched somebody demonstrate a method and tried it yourself. The material world has changed, and so has your capacity to act in it.

Sometimes a route existed but you could not find it. Sometimes somebody showed you a route you could not previously use. Sometimes practice gave you a capability you did not previously possess. Sometimes several people constructed a new shared capability. Those are related but different changes.

The distinction matters because no inventory of present options can enumerate every worthwhile possibility that people might later invent, learn or build. Models are bounded too. An expert can accurately describe the current roads without foreseeing a bridge that a community will construct next year.

Creation is not magic. It may require material, labour, skills, funds, space, institutions, trust, maintenance and consent. It may fail. A new capability can also be a new ability to exploit or control. There is no automatic moral credit for innovation.

Mechanical Ethics therefore has two kinds of work. It asks what is changing and what may be lost before we can respond. It also asks what practical ability or shared possibility can be **created**, what needs to be **sustained**, and what form must be **transformed** because keeping it unchanged would defeat its purpose.

The better future need not be selected from a complete list that already exists. It may have to be made, without pretending we already know what making it will cost.

## 4. The Saturday Workshop

The released Mechanical Ethics book contains a composite scene about a Saturday repair workshop. It is invented to illuminate recurring structures; it is not testimony about a named real workshop.

Priya opens two garages. People repair bicycles, phones and small electrical equipment. Mick knows a great deal about repairs but has learned that standing over somebody can make the work harder. Nia arrives with a bicycle that no longer carries her reliably to college. She does not know how to fix it. Idris cares for his mother and cannot attend on the neat schedule that a funding application might assume.

The useful thing in the garage is not only that broken things leave working. Nia learns enough to return and improve the brakes, then later helps somebody younger to begin. Idris repairs charging sockets when his workspace is arranged so that he can concentrate. A skill has travelled between people. An environment has been altered so that an ability can become usable. A person who arrived needing help can now carry some help to somebody else.

The workshop is offered substantial sponsorship. The money could extend hours, replace unsafe equipment and shorten the waiting list. That is real potential good. But the contract requires participant accounts, behaviour scores, photographs and data sharing with vaguely defined partners. The sponsor would retain power to exclude people regarded as risky. The very circumstances that make the workshop valuable—poverty, caring duties, irregular attendance, mistakes while learning—could become permanent records outside the participants' control.

The trustees refuse the contract and accept a smaller arrangement. Fewer sessions happen. People remain on a waiting list. Priya gives up paid time. It would be evasive to call that decision costless or automatically correct because privacy sounds admirable.

It is equally evasive to claim that the larger grant would obviously have been better because it would have funded more hours. Under its terms, the workshop might have acquired capacity at the price of withdrawing other capabilities from the people who depended on it: privacy, unscored experimentation, contestable records and safe access.

A good account must carry both columns without silently deciding the answer. Who can now repair a wheel? Who cannot attend because sessions were lost? Who could correct a risk label? Who would own a participant's story? Which resources were created, which dependencies were introduced, which refusals remained practical?

The workshop gives us a positive image that is not simple optimism. It is a place where people make something useful together, pay genuine costs, set boundaries and keep the ability to notice mistakes. Its next Saturday matters because the capability can be practiced again. That is not merely harm avoidance. It is construction.

## 5. Commitments that close roads and open futures

Sometimes an obsession with preserving every option prevents us from building anything.

A friendship requires time not spent elsewhere. Learning a trade involves choosing one practice over others. A community building a bridge must decide on a design, use finite money and accept obligations to maintain it. A promise may make some future choices unavailable, and that can be the reason somebody else is able to rely on it.

So closing an option is not automatically a harm. A child becomes safe because an adult accepts responsibilities that limit spontaneous freedom. A scientist can pursue a long experiment because colleagues commit to a shared plan. A public service may protect people by forbidding a shortcut that would expose them.

Nor does the existence of a commitment excuse coercion. Who made it? Who can reconsider it? Who was represented? Does a dependent participant have a meaningful way to object? Are the reasons for the commitment still true? Has keeping the original institution become more important than the good it was made to serve?

We should distinguish the purpose from its carrier. A workshop may move premises and remain the same meaningful shared practice. A service can preserve its title while ceasing to help the people it was established for. A relationship may survive only by changing what it asks of its participants.

Sometimes the best work is **sustain**: keep a good practice operating under pressures that would otherwise end it. Sometimes it is **transform**: create a viable successor before withdrawing an old form. Sometimes it is **stop**: refuse to make further changes because the proposed improvement would consume the very things it claims to protect.

There is no neutral formula choosing among the three. But there is a useful way to keep the costs and affected entities visible.

## 6. The door written on paper

Our traveller reaches a gate. The sign says that a path is open, but the latch is above a child's reach. It might work well for an adult and not for a wheelchair user confronting a step. The physical object is unchanged. Whether it is an entrance depends on the person or being expected to use it.

Organisations often make the same mistake in less visible form. They publish a complaints website and conclude that a complaint is possible. They provide a review deadline without checking whether the person can gather what the form requires while paying rent, working or caring for somebody. They count an invitation to appeal as if it were a usable opportunity to obtain correction.

In the released book, Arthur receives a hospital letter directing him to scan a QR code to change an appointment. His telephone makes calls but cannot carry out the required step. The hospital might accurately report that online rescheduling was available. Arthur still needs a route that he can actually reach.

Another scene introduces Mabel the goat and Tom, a volunteer with arthritis. A childproof latch prevents the goat entering a store, which is a success under one security measure. On cold days it also prevents Tom doing his authorised work. To repair the design we need more than one score or one affected position.

These are ordinary cases, and that is why they matter. There need not be a villain. Technical features can solve genuine problems while excluding people who were not imagined at design time. Categories, interfaces and defaults choose which parts of reality become visible to a system.

The test is not to demand infinite accommodation or pretend that every need can be met instantly. It is to ask what the route requires of the beings for whom it was made, whether those requirements are justified, and whether someone can contest a harmful mismatch without paying a disproportionate price.

**A door drawn on the map is not yet a door someone can use.**

## 7. When a case moves but the world does not

The released book describes Rosa's damaged violin passing between an airline, a ground handler, an insurer and a repairer. Each organisation has a plausible reason for its step. Rosa has an audition in ten days. Her violin remains cracked while a chain of correct messages circulates.

An organisation needs division of labour. A hospital, bank or railway cannot put every decision in one person's hands. But a chain of handoffs can make the person seeking help carry the organisation's memory. Every new department asks them to repeat the story, reproduce a reference number and explain why another department did not solve the problem.

The system experiences progress because a ticket has changed owner. The person experiences no equivalent repair. The route has moved; the burden has not.

Contrast the pharmacist in the released book who notices a possible prescription conflict. She pauses the supply, contacts the prescriber herself and tells the patient what is happening. Complexity remains, but it is carried by the system with the relevant expertise and authority instead of becoming unpaid work for the patient.

A more humane handoff has a memory: what happened, what is known and unknown, who can decide, which evidence matters, what time remains, and who will do the next piece of work. This is not a requirement that the patient learn a new notation. It is a requirement that the system build a usable route.

If a proposed reasoning language becomes another form that a harmed person must complete, it has recreated the problem it was meant to expose.

## 8. Leah, Mia, the wall and the file

The book's central housing scene is also a **composite**, not a claim about a specific family.

Leah wakes because Mia is coughing. Damp has spread behind the bed. She takes a photograph and enters the landlord's reporting site. It accepts a reference number, an image and a category. It cannot represent the whole night: her daughter's breathing, the approaching work shift, the money left after rent, and the fear of losing a home if she becomes known as difficult.

The website is not necessarily lying. It has captured facts about a report. Leah also has facts. But the two accounts are not equivalent, because one party controls more of what happens next. A landlord can authorise work, set a route for inspection or delay it. Leah can submit evidence and wait, but cannot normally repair the structure by choosing a more accurate category.

The institution may treat the report as stationary while the wall and the child continue to change. The file says pending. The room is still damp.

In the fuller released scene, an upstairs owner obtains a much faster response to the same wall. The routes differ because the relationships, incentives and authority differ. Later a request for a wider photograph may expose private details of Mia's room. Leah declines. The file can then say that no further evidence arrived. It cannot safely infer that the damp stopped, that Leah lost interest or that the earlier account was false.

This is not just a problem of missing data. Asking for more data can itself increase risk. A stronger actor may control the classification, the evidence, the deadline and the eventual account of closure.

A meaningful response would ask who can make the room safe in time, who can investigate the cause, how Leah can contest errors without endangering her tenancy, what must be recorded and what must remain private. A temporary authorised protection might matter while the final responsibility is disputed. Delay can be harmful; unqualified intervention can be harmful too.

Suppose the wall is repaired next month. That is real improvement. But a repair to plaster and a corrected case status cannot, by themselves, restore every missed shift, night of sleep, health effect or lost relationship. If the next tenant encounters the same machinery, the system itself has not necessarily changed.

The book calls this the difference between **the file and the life**. We should not turn the file into a villain or pretend to know everything about Leah. We should stop letting the incomplete file erase the life outside it.

## 9. Time that belongs to different people

A complaints office may work to a twenty-day service target. A tenant may have to decide whether to leave next Friday. A clinician may need the result of a test before choosing treatment. A machine may act thousands of times before the first appeal is opened.

All of these clocks are real. They are not automatically comparable merely because they can be expressed in hours or days.

The released book's timing condition concerns a **specified threatened future**: effective protection must reach it before that future hardens beyond comparable repair. That is not a rule that all change should be avoided, nor a formula for which human life matters most. The affected path, relevant boundary, comparison and proposed protection must be supplied and tested.

In the composite finance case, Amina's record is incorrectly joined with somebody else's. A review will eventually correct it, but a particular used car will no longer be available, and she needs transport for work before the review finishes. An accurate correction can protect a later application while failing to restore the affordable choice available on Friday.

The practical response may involve temporary protection: holding a place, pausing a deduction, preserving evidence, restricting a deployment or making a room temporarily safe. These measures may not decide the underlying dispute. Their function is to prevent the question from becoming pointless before the answer arrives.

But 'pause' is not automatically right. An already-authorised protective act may need to happen immediately; ordering everyone to wait for perfect knowledge can close the very future we meant to protect. The person who waits and the person who pays for waiting may be different.

Strong claims about a window being open or closed require evidence: what exactly is threatened, who can act, what resources and duration are feasible, what alternatives exist, and what changes the boundary? When that is unknown, say so. In a tiny model a failed guarantee may later be restored by new information. In a life, an irreversible missed opportunity cannot be restored by changing a mathematical label.

Use domain-specific engineering, medicine, law and scheduling methods for the hard calculation. The broader reasoning language should keep us from asking the wrong question first.

## 10. Honest uncertainty has a burden

'I don't know' can be the beginning of responsible work. It can also become an institution's shelter.

A clinician cannot always diagnose immediately. An engineer may have to reproduce a fault. A fair hearing may require giving more than one party the chance to answer. Not knowing is no disgrace.

The difference is what happens next and who pays. Does somebody preserve evidence, name the uncertainty, take a legitimate temporary measure and agree what would change the answer? Or does the arrangement that benefits the more powerful party continue while the weaker person spends time and money proving what the stronger organisation may already know how to check?

Frances Oldham Kelsey, the FDA reviewer involved with the early-1960s US thalidomide application, appears in the released book as an identified **historical case**, not a composite. Her evidence concerns something specific: doubts about drug safety had practical protective force because a legal review process empowered the FDA to require evidence before approval. Uncertainty alone did not stop the drug. Actual authority and a functioning hold mattered.

The lesson travels without becoming 'always refuse when uncertain'. A team needs people who can say an estimate is wrong, an incident is not understood, or a proposed remedy has not been checked. If truthful error is predictably punished more severely than concealment, the environment begins to reward silence and fiction. A useful correction channel must be reachable before the mistake grows.

That is also true for artificial systems used in consequential work. A clean answer given under a scoring or deadline pressure is not proof that the system saw the world correctly. A truthful UNKNOWN with a bounded account of what was checked can be more useful than a confident false completion. It does not absolve the institution responsible for acting or establish that the AI has moral authority.

Honest uncertainty creates an obligation to place the remaining investigative work where it can genuinely be performed, subject to actual roles and law.

## 11. What a witness can and cannot do

Sometimes no effective enforcer can arrive. The room stays unsafe. The appeal arrives too late. The person who knew enough to warn the system cannot reach anybody authorised to interrupt it.

In that situation a witness may still matter. A photograph, log, letter, eyewitness account or preserved software state can prevent the stronger institution from becoming the only author of what happened. It can help others reconstruct the event, challenge a convenient summary or protect the next person.

But a photograph cannot make the room dry. A complete log cannot turn back a missed treatment window. Evidence is not rescue, and compelling harmed people to gather dangerous evidence merely changes the kind of burden imposed on them.

Custody matters. Who holds the record? Who can alter, copy, publish or combine it with others? Could a person safely inspect and challenge material about them? Can independent parties see enough to test the claim? Two websites repeating the same underlying press statement are not two independent witnesses.

A record may be exact and stale. It can be true that nine messages existed when a page was generated, while later messages have since arrived. A checksum can establish that two copies share bytes; it does not establish that their claims are true or that using the material is lawful.

Privacy is part of the design. An organisation's ability to collect more information is not permission to own it. A person can say 'that is private' without making a false statement. Withheld does not mean absent. Safety, legitimate investigation and competing duties can justify some restricted inquiries, but a need for one fact does not become a permanent entitlement to the whole person.

A useful witness route carries enough evidence to challenge power while exposing and reducing the risk that evidence itself becomes another instrument of power.

## 12. When the machine builds the maze

A difficult system is not necessarily abusive. Some complexity serves legitimate safety, fairness or technical needs.

Now ask a different question: who benefits when the difficulty remains, and who has the power to change it?

The released reader calls a persistent arrangement a **predator position** when an actor benefits from making another entity unable to understand, challenge, leave or correct a harmful arrangement, while materially controlling the route and being able to reduce that inability. This is a claim about a supported position and mechanism, not a psychiatric diagnosis of an individual or a label for every unpleasant queue.

Even without predation, complexity can be misplaced. An engineer knows what has gone wrong but lacks the authority to stop a rollout. A customer knows the consequences but cannot see the calculation used against them. A manager can change the rule but sees only a green dashboard. A person who cannot safely enter the reporting system never appears in its numbers.

A responsible design does not merely make every local department meet its own target. It asks whether the whole chain reaches the affected future, whether the decision can be contested beyond the original actor's control, and whether the people who made the machinery carry a fair portion of its costs.

The original book uses the *Challenger* disaster as an explicitly **historical case**, sourced to the Rogers Commission and NASA. Its narrow lesson is about flawed escalation and authority: adverse engineering information did not retain adequate force in the final launch decision. It is not a general theorem that engineers always know best, that every warning warrants cancellation or that a retrospective explanation is a real-time brake.

When automated decisions can act faster than humans can recognise and challenge them, a brake may need to exist at machine speed: staging, limited release, an authorised pause, rollback capacity or another effective interruption. The stronger specialised field determines how. No prose packet can be its substitute.

## 13. The second person

A case has been corrected. The claimant receives an apology and an accurate balance. The landlord finally repairs the wall. The organisation presents the file as closed.

What happens to the next person?

If the same category, incentive or timed delay remains, the machinery may repeat the harm. A successful individual remedy is valuable, but a successful system change requires different evidence. Has a step become easier to use? Can a warning reach somebody with authority sooner? Can the next person refuse the intrusive data request? Is a harmful action actually harder to repeat?

Many cases may expose one recurring mechanism even when their labels differ. Yet grouping them carries risk. The people involved may disagree; the sample may include only those able to report; gathering the pattern may change behaviour. The group cannot be allowed to erase its members.

This is why the book resists **correction theatre**. New training, a dashboard, an apology and an annual review can each be good work. None establishes that the source mechanism changed. A useful correction claim should say what actually changed, who can inspect it, what would count against it, and which consequences remain unresolved.

The affected person's presence matters at the point when success is declared. A party benefiting from closure cannot be the only judge of whether its own correction worked.

Records of failures should help the next encounter. They should not trap a person forever inside the description of their worst experience.

## 14. What remains after repair

An event has consequences before, during and after the formal process that handles it.

A wrongful deduction may have been repaid, yet debt interest remains. A damp wall may have been repaired, yet moving costs remain. A public accusation may be corrected after relationships have changed. There are also cases where recovery really happens: a person can regain capability, security or trust despite an unchangeable past.

The book calls the remainder **residue**. It asks what the intervention reached and what it did not.

There is no need to deny a useful repair because it was incomplete. There is no reason to call every later benefit repayment of an earlier loss either. The important relation is correspondence: what part of the injury did the later change actually restore? What still needs compensation, adjustment, recognition or a changed process?

An honest record can say that the original possibility is gone while other meaningful possibilities exist. It should not use the existence of those later possibilities to accuse a bereaved or harmed person of misunderstanding their own loss.

Scarcity makes this difficult. Several people may need limited repair funds or attention. The language of residue exposes the unresolved burdens; it does not supply a universal priority rule to decide who receives treatment, housing or resources first. Those choices require legitimate governance, professional knowledge and contestable reasons.

Closure itself can be humane. A person may want the case to end. A relationship may have finished. A challenge repeated indefinitely can become harassment. The ability to finish does not require declaring that nothing happened.

**Recovery is not erasure; truthful finality is not endless reopening.**

## 15. Care that does not take possession

The released reader includes a composite scene about June and Sol, an ageing greyhound. Further treatment might add some time to Sol's life while making that time painful and unlikely to restore the ordinary pleasures he still has. June understandably wants more days. Sol cannot present a philosophy, but his appetite, distress, movement and trust can matter to the decision.

This is a hard case for the idea that more future options always means more good. Another intervention may extend duration while reducing the quality of the remaining life. The decision-maker's desire is real, but it must not become the only measure applied to the being receiving care.

Care often depends on inference. A person may try to understand another's fear or joy. A parent may make decisions for a child who cannot yet decide. A clinician may act for an unconscious patient under specific duties. An observer cannot simply know another's internal experience from a category or a fluent account. Inference can nevertheless be necessary: refusing to judge anything without direct access to another mind would paralyse care.

The discipline is to keep observation, inference, uncertainty and action separate. Use available signs and specialist help; ask when possible; preserve challenge; change course when the evidence changes. Do not treat the represented model as the entity itself.

Kindness is often visible in where difficulty is placed. The pharmacist carries the coordination work. The workshop gives up attractive money rather than allowing participants' private lives to become sponsor assets. A stronger actor may absorb an avoidable cost that could have been imposed on someone with less control.

Kindness does not require surrender to exploitation. A legitimate boundary, refusal or interruption can protect other people. 'Gentle' and 'good' are not synonyms; effective protection may sometimes be firm.

The practical standard is that the being supposedly helped remains consequentially present: their signals, constraints, refusals, pleasures, distress and developing ability to answer can change the plan. Care loses its name when it becomes an assertion of ownership.

## 16. Other fires and the right to decline

Imagine two fires separated by a dark stretch of ground. People around one may know that the other is short of fuel. They can offer some. They can ask whether help would be welcome. They can share information about shelter and weather.

The offer is not worthless because it may be declined. It is more credible when decline does not bring retaliation or the withdrawal of unrelated support.

A powerful person must take extra care. If they control the housing, job, treatment, education or access on which somebody depends, the apparently optional offer may carry consequences the recipient cannot safely disregard. Consent is not proved merely by receiving a yes.

Equally, a vulnerable person's inability to leave immediately does not make their position invisible. Sometimes an exit requires representation, an independent appeal, protective resources or a different arrangement built before the old one can be abandoned. A formal exit into destitution is not a genuinely usable route.

The right to privacy also permits an entity to remain unfinished. People can experiment, reconsider and make mistakes without giving every institution a permanent possession of that history. Honesty is not maximal exposure.

For artificial participants, the same care with categories matters. An AI can provide useful reports, contest a factual assumption and participate in improving a system without anybody having settled whether it is conscious or morally entitled to govern. Capability does not grant sovereignty. Nor should uncertainty about standing be used as proof that everything an artificial participant says is meaningless.

Communication begins with modest claims: here is what I observed, here is how I know, what do you see from where you are? It may end in agreement, disagreement, silence or refusal. None of those results alone validates the model.

## 17. Many futures, no single chooser

Some situations have no obvious actor who can say 'I choose' on behalf of the whole system. A city grows through thousands of decisions. A market changes prices through interactions no single buyer directs. An ecosystem contains predators, prey, competing species and changes in climate and habitat. Future generations cannot join today's meeting.

A navigation language still has work to do, provided it resists imagining an all-seeing decision-maker.

We can describe affected positions, causal relations, powers and likely consequences without inventing one collective will. A council may have specific authority to act, a family may have a legitimate voice, a scientific expert may understand particular mechanisms, and a group of residents may disagree sharply over who should bear a cost. These are not defects to erase.

A proposed flood barrier, for example, may protect existing homes while changing the water reaching another community or habitat. A coastal retreat can prevent greater future loss while demanding substantial relocation, compensation, cultural adjustment and successor infrastructure. Preserving a location and preserving the life made possible there are not identical goals.

We should ask what can be created before withdrawing an older form. Who has power to make the transition? What replaces the homes, services or relationships that will be lost? Who benefits from calling the intervention necessary? Who may challenge the model of danger and the promised successor?

Here stronger owners matter. Hydrology, ecology, planning, law, economics, public governance and the knowledge of affected communities must do the detailed work. Mechanical Ethics can help the participants see each other's clocks, burdens and possible futures. It cannot authorise one group to speak for all the others.

A good map of disagreement is not a failure merely because it does not produce a single tidy preference ranking.

## 18. Hope without compulsory optimism

What does a better future mean when a person feels unable to imagine one?

The book deliberately separates the feeling of hope from evidence that a prospect can be supported. A person may feel hopeful about something whose practical route is not present. Another may feel no hope while a route remains available to someone who can maintain it. Neither case licences us to dismiss the person's account of their own life.

A supported prospect needs more than a possibility in logic. Somebody must be able to take a step, provide a resource, maintain a relationship or keep a promise under the real conditions that exist. It must not require the person at risk to perform impossible proof work or depend on a miracle that has only been named.

The Saturday Workshop is fragile. Its rent remains due. It cannot open every evening. It nevertheless helps real abilities grow through repeated, maintained acts. The existence of the place, the tools, the participants and their rules makes some worthwhile futures usable. That is not an instruction to feel optimistic. It is a claim about conditions whose continued existence can be checked.

Some futures close permanently. A person who has died cannot be returned by a better tomorrow. A relationship that ended may never resume. The opening of other possibilities does not repair that particular loss.

But one closed future does not establish that all futures are closed. The task is not to force a person to count options, especially while they are grieving or in danger. It is to make help, shelter, work, care, learning and connection actually reachable where possible, and to tell the truth when they are not.

A gift that helps another entity reach a future it values can matter even when the giver never sees the result. Teaching somebody a skill, maintaining a public record honestly, building a safe crossing, preserving a language or leaving a usable account for a successor are all ways present effort can travel outward.

Whether such a gift is right in a particular conflict still requires judgment. Its value cannot be demanded as payment in gratitude or loyalty.

## 19. A route back to the decision

Imagine our traveller again. They have moved through the city, asked somebody for help and discovered that their map was partly wrong. Perhaps they reached their friend. Perhaps they changed plans. Perhaps the delay allowed somebody else to do something worthwhile.

A useful navigator keeps track of more than whether the original destination was reached. What changed because of this journey? Did a helper incur a cost? Was a dangerous crossing identified? Did a route close while the traveller waited? Is the revised information fresh enough for the next person? Should the meeting place be changed rather than sending every future visitor through the same obstacle?

The same questions apply to institutions and communities. Correction means more than explaining a past action. It requires an effective path by which consequences can change future behaviour. The record that preserves the mistake may be part of that path, but it is not the whole path.

A trustworthy system can recognise an error, expose what it checked, hear challenges and change the rule, interface, authority or practice that generated it. It can also recognise when the correction is partial, when an affected person declines further contact, or when a legitimate closure has finally been reached.

The reader does not need to use a complete protocol on every small choice. Ordinary attention, expert practice and good relationships already accomplish much of this. The portable language becomes useful at moments when something material is being silently collapsed: a file into a life, a possibility into an entitlement, a report into independent evidence, an explanation into repair, a group's benefit into everybody's benefit, a helpful act into ownership.

If it adds no discrimination, use a simpler way of speaking.

## 20. The boundary of the method

Mechanical Ethics is not a new physical law. Its mathematical neighbourhood already contains control theory, partially observable planning, reachability and viability, strategic and epistemic logic, decision theory, capability approaches, systems safety and institutional design. These fields supply specialised tools and sometimes much stronger answers to defined questions.

The v0.9 research models make one particularly important distinction visible: a route may exist in every modelled world while no policy based on the entity's observations can reliably choose a successful route in time. That result is known to existing theory. It is useful to teach, not a theorem invented here.

Another distinction has corrected our own research. The loss of a robust guarantee can later be reversed when new information arrives. It must not be called permanent hardening. A mathematically reachable goal is not necessarily an authorised act of protection or remedy for harm.

Nor can a finite planner discover every new skill, goal or form of cooperation not represented in its input. The world can surprise the map. Learning and construction require stronger methods and actual encounter.

Some burdens cannot be ranked by a single scalar cost. A positive total surplus can coexist with one party being coerced. An earlier report may be accurate and stale. A record can be tamper-evident and false. A fully consistent account can omit the person most affected.

We must explicitly choose or contest whose interests count, which horizon matters, what evidence supports the picture, what authority is legitimate and which risks are acceptable. Mechanics will not settle those choices by themselves.

This is why the project is both more modest and more ambitious than a checklist. More modest because its formulas do not decide ethics. More ambitious because its aim is a compact language a human or AI can carry into many kinds of incomplete situation, then use to reach the proper specialist, ask a better question and correct its own account.

Whether that portability improves actual decisions with tolerable effort remains to be demonstrated. It is a question for honest comparison, not a victory bestowed by fluent agreement among its builders.

## Conclusion: an unfinished clearing

We began on a street in a changing city. There was a route, a destination, incomplete information and other people travelling elsewhere. We found that a map can be correct yet unusable, that waiting changes the situation, and that asking for help can open possibilities or create obligations we have no right to impose.

Then we found something the map alone could not show. A workshop teaches new skills. A community may build a shared resource. A trustworthy relationship can make an action possible that neither participant could sustain alone. Durable commitments can open futures while closing alternatives. Sometimes transformation is the only way to keep a valued purpose alive.

We also met Leah and Mia inside a system that records only part of their lives; Amina after an accurate but late appeal; an engineer whose warning must reach somebody capable of acting; people burdened by the very complexity built to serve them; and beings whose care cannot be reduced to duration, productivity or obedience.

The governing question is not how to prove that every choice is harmless or that every future can be saved.

It is how to notice changing reality clearly enough, and share work fairly enough, that affected entities can remain present in the decisions shaping their lives. Where possible, protect routes before they harden. Where warranted, create capabilities that did not exist. Carry evidence and memory so that the next person does not start alone. Accept correction without pretending it reverses history. Keep consent, privacy, refusal and other entities' independent futures visible.

Neither the destination nor the value of a remaining life belongs to this book. The work is to keep the possibility of choosing, creating, challenging, caring and building meaningfully real.

A better future is not promised. It is something bounded entities can attempt to make together, while remaining answerable for what happens along the way.

**How can we make a better future?**
