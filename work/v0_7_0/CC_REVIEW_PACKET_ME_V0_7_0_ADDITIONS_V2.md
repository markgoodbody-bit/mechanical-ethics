## Proposal 1 — The Door Out

**Placement:** after Chapter 3, before Part II — The Clock.

```text
## The Door Out

A system may say that a person was free to refuse or leave. That claim depends on what using the exit would cost.

A worker can resign and lose the income needed for housing. A tenant can move and lose money, stability, school continuity, or access to evidence. A patient can decline a condition and lose the treatment attached to it. The formal option exists, but it may exchange one controlled harm for another loss the person cannot survive.

A meaningful exit leaves at least one viable continuation outside the arrangement. The person can understand that refusal is possible, exercise it without retaliation or disproportionate danger, keep essential records and possessions, and reach another route before the loss hardens. This does not make exit costless. It distinguishes a door from a penalty described as choice.

Some relationships offer no safe immediate exit. A child cannot simply leave a family, a patient may depend on urgent treatment, and a person under restraint may have almost no practical refusal. In those cases greater weight falls on independent interruption, protected representation, review, and limits on what the stronger party may demand.

A door out deserves moral credit only when using it does not make refusal the mechanism of harm.
```

## Proposal 2 — Distributed responsibility

**Placement:** Chapter 10, after the paragraph stating that authority and information may be separated by design.

```text
Responsibility can be distributed without disappearing. The designer who set the categories, the manager who maintained the incentive, the custodian who controlled the record, and the executive who could interrupt the process may stand in different relations to the same outcome. Their duties need not be identical, but fragmented control cannot become an organisational claim that nobody is answerable for the arrangement as a whole.
```

## Proposal 3 — When One Case Is Not Enough

**Placement:** after Chapter 11 — The Second Person, before Chapter 12 — Correction Theatre.

```text
## When One Case Is Not Enough

One case can expose a mechanism and still be dismissed as an exception. Several accounts may show the same route recurring even when the surface labels differ.

Collective evidence does not require every account to be identical. People may disagree about cause, remedy, or severity while still showing that one design repeatedly places the same burden or closes the same kind of path. Combining their records should preserve those differences rather than flattening them into one representative story.

Representation can give scattered people enough weight to reach authority, but it creates another custody question: who selects the accounts, speaks for the group, controls the record, and decides what can be exposed? Where safety permits, people should be able to inspect and challenge claims made in their name.

Frequency is not automatic authority. A common pattern can be misunderstood, and a rare event can reveal severe harm. The practical gain is that people no longer have to begin from zero when separate accounts point to a shared mechanism. Collective correction occurs when the combined account can change the rule, incentive, interface, or authority that keeps reproducing the harm.

The group does not erase the person. It gives the person's account a route that may finally carry enough weight to reach the machine.
```

## Proposal 4 — Human closing paragraph

**Placement:** end of the Conclusion, immediately before the Author's Note.

```text
In ordinary terms, the route must reach the person in the conditions they actually face. Those with greater power should carry the work of uncertainty, evidence, explanation, and temporary protection rather than export it to the person at risk. Correction should be judged by whether it can still alter the affected life, not only whether it can amend the record. When comparable repair is no longer possible, the account should say what closed, who still carries the consequence, and what must change before the next case. A responsible system need not promise perfect outcomes. It must remain usable, challengeable, interruptible, and honest about what it could not repair.
```
