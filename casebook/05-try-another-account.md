# Try another account

These short exercises invite you to make a judgement before reading the authors'
interpretations. They are optional. A useful answer can disagree with the book,
ask for missing evidence, or conclude that an existing method is sufficient.

The decision situations below are constructed for learning. They do not put you
in command of an agency, a live emergency or a real employer.

## 1. What would make a check convincing?

Before reading the institutional-trust chapter, imagine reviewing a proposed
information-transfer system. Its agreement specifies conditions for release.
Its software confirms that required fields contain something. A manager signs
off the completed run.

Would you describe that as a compliant transfer? What single piece of additional
evidence would most change your confidence? Name a condition the system might
fail to establish even though its checks report success.

Now read [the actual case](01-institutional-trust.md), especially sections 4,
9 and 14. Distinguish a deficiency in a request from a deficiency in validation.
Then make the strongest case against your initial answer. A blanket refusal can
also fail an applicable duty; the framework cannot settle that duty for you.

The learning question is whether your account now distinguishes a stated
condition, a performed check and evidence that the condition actually holds.
There is no score for reaching the author's political or moral preferences.

## 2. Where does the protective journey end?

Before the fire chapter, sketch a journey from a warning to protection. Choose
the endpoint you are claiming: a message received, movement begun, reduced
immediate exposure, or arrival somewhere suitable. What must connect the
starting event to that endpoint?

Change one assumption: an outward route becomes unavailable. Which parts of
your account still hold, and which would require competent local assessment?
Do not select a refuge location or invent a safe time margin.

Read [the Camp Fire case](02-camp-fire.md). Identify one revision you would make
and one question the chapter cannot answer. The aim is to recognise where your
account needs specialist evidence, not to acquire emergency-planning authority
from a reading exercise.

## 3. What would you actually offer?

Before the employment chapter, imagine a small firm with useful beginner work,
money for wages and a qualified supervisor whose spare time is uncertain. An
adult newcomer wants a paid opportunity.

What could the firm honestly offer now? Consider more than one course: redesign
the work, provide supervisor cover, use an appropriate training route, or delay
the offer. State one fact that would make you change your preference. Do not
invent a budget or treat the newcomer as obliged to accept your design.

Read [the paid-first-rung case](03-paid-first-rung.md). Answer once from the
newcomer's position and once from the supervisor's. Does either perspective
expose a cost your first account assigned to someone else? What would make the
opportunity worth creating rather than merely administratively permissible?

## Keep one revision

You need not fill in a report. Keep one sentence if useful: “I first assumed …;
after considering …, I would now …; I still need to know …”. If nothing changed,
say why. That can expose either a strong initial account or a weak exercise.

These exercises have not been tried with outside readers. They make the
casebook's reasoning easier to contest; they do not establish that its teaching
works. Their next improvement should follow what a reader actually misunderstands
or finds useful, rather than an arbitrary demand for more questions.
